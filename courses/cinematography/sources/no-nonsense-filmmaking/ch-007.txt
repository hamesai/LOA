Creati ve Control 
Light is something to be sculpted and manipulated according to the emotional needs of your project. 
Consider the following emotions: 
• fear 
• relief 
• suspicion 
• joy 
How might you use light to convey them? 
There is no right answer. 
Let’s use th e emotion of f ear as an e xample. You might create abject terror by plunging your scene 
into the darkest shadows and obscuring what the audience can see. However, depending on the type of 
story you are telling, it is also conceivable that bright, piercing light might be more effective. 
As with any aspect of filmmaking, if the results work, then that is all that matters. 
There are four distinct ways in which you can control light. 
 
Quality 
The quality of light can vary. Think about the light on a sunny afternoon with no clouds in the 
sky. Compare that to another day in which the clouds have rolled in and hidden the sun. In what 
ways does the light coming from the same source change? 
1. Direct light has a clear and uninterrupted path from its source. It is notable for casting 
defined shadows. This sort of light also has a very concentrated spread. 
2. Diffused light is scattered and soft, a result of something obscuring and diffusing the 
source. It is notable for its lack of defined shadows and its even distribution across a 
subject. It is sometimes referred to as “soft” light.

Intensity 
The intensity or amount of light changes depending on its source. 
Consider the amount of light emitted by a stadium spotlight. Compare that to the nightlight 
by your bed. Compare that to the sun. 
Each light source generates a different intensity, which can be manipulated. 
You can change the intensity of a light source in several ways. 
◦ Decrease or increase the intensity. This might be done by using a dimmer or 
switching out a bulb with a lower or higher wattage. This is not something you can do 
with the sun, for obvious reasons. 
◦ Place something in front of the light to cut the intensity. This may involve using 
various grip equipment or a neutral density filter. Either way, you are placing a lightcutting element between the light source and the image processor. 
◦ Move the light closer to or farther away from your subject. 
◦ Move the subject closer to or farther away from your light source. 
This is a good time to revisit the subject of f-stops. 
As you may recall, th e f-stop on y our camera is an in dication of th e size of th e aperture. It 
controls how much light is hitting the image processor. 
Depending on the intensity of your light source, you will need to adjust your f-stop to obtain 
the correct exposure. Furthermore, depending on th e depth of fi eld you are hoping to a chieve, 
you may need to adjust the intensity of the light to achieve the required f-stop. 
The f-stop you use is a creative decision. Manipulate the intensity of the light source to match 
your f-stop goal. 
Color 
What color is light? It all depends on how you and the camera perceive it. Remember, it’s a matter 
of color temperature. 
The simplest way to control the color of a light is to put som ething in front of it. Use colored 
gels to rapidly change the color temperature of your light source. 
NOTE: Be very careful when you put anything in front of a light. If using gels, only use special film gels that are 
designed to withstand the heat generated by the light source. Using something else can cause a fire!

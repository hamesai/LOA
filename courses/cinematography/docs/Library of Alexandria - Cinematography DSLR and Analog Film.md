## Module 1: Exposure for motion: stops, frame rate and shutter angle

Prerequisites: None. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Every exposure choice is also a motion choice. Count in stops, set the shutter angle for the blur you want, and land brightness with aperture, ISO and ND filters rather than by changing the shutter. Know what slow motion and rolling shutter cost.

### Tutorial

1. Learn the stop scale for aperture, shutter and ISO.
2. Set 24 fps at about 1/48 s (180°) and test blur at other speeds.
3. Choose aperture for depth of field, then land exposure with ISO and ND.

Worked example: Sunny scene correct at f/11 at ISO 100 and 1/50 s. To shoot at f/2.8 you need four stops of ND (density 1.2).

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** At 25 fps you want a shutter angle of 144°. What shutter speed does that give, and which common speed is nearest?
- Answer key: (144 ÷ 360) ÷ 25 = 0.4 ÷ 25 = 1/62.5 s. The nearest common speed is 1/60 s, which is 150°.
- Self-check: Did you divide the angle by 360 first, then by the frame rate?

**C2 (core).** A scene reads f/16 at ISO 100 and 1/50 s. You want f/2.8. How many stops of ND, and what density would you pick?
- Answer key: f/16 to f/2.8 is f/16, 11, 8, 5.6, 4, 2.8: five stops. Five stops is a factor of 32, so density 1.5. If only 1.2 and 1.8 exist, use 1.8 and close to about f/4.
- Self-check: Count the steps on the scale, then check that 10 to the density is close to 2 to the stops.

**C3 (stretch).** Shoot the same hand movement three times at 24 fps: 1/48, 1/125 and 1/500. Then shoot it at 60 fps with 1/120. Write one sentence for each clip about blur and how you would use it.
- Answer key: Blur should shrink as the speed rises: smooth at 1/48, crisper at 1/125, near-frozen at 1/500. The 60 fps clip, played at 24 fps, should look 40% speed.
- Self-check: Step through the clips frame by frame. In which is the hand most smeared?

### Common mistakes

- Leaving the shutter on 1/1000 for 'sharpness' → record at about 180° unless you want a stutter look.
- Using aperture to fix exposure in a scene → use ND, ISO or lights and keep the aperture you chose.
- Forgetting the light cost of slow motion → add up the stops before the day.

## Module 2: The DSLR as a cine camera: sensors, profiles, sound and limits

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
A DSLR gives a large sensor and a low price, and it brings limits: clip length, sound, rolling shutter and profile choices. Know your own camera's limits, work out crop factor and equivalent lenses, and record sound and exposure with a plan.

### Tutorial

1. Find your camera's real limits (clip length, bit rate, frame rates, mic input).
2. Work out crop-factor equivalents for your lenses.
3. Set a profile on purpose, and protect highlights with an exposure aid.

Worked example: A 50 mm full-frame framing on a 1.6 crop camera needs about 31 mm. A 45 Mb/s camera uses about 337 MB per minute, so a 64 GB card holds about 190 minutes.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Your camera has a crop factor of 1.6. Which lens gives the field of view of a 50 mm full-frame lens? And what does your 24 mm lens frame like on full frame?
- Answer key: 50 ÷ 1.6 = 31.25 mm, so about 30 to 32 mm. 24 × 1.6 = 38.4 mm.
- Self-check: Did you divide for 'match this framing' and multiply for 'what is this lens like on full frame'?

**C2 (core).** A camera records at 45 Mb/s. How many MB per minute? How many minutes fit on a 64 GB card (64,000 MB)? How long before it reaches a 4 GB file cap?
- Answer key: 45 ÷ 8 = 5.625 MB/s, so 337.5 MB per minute. 64,000 ÷ 337.5 ≈ 190 minutes. 4096 MB ÷ 5.625 ≈ 728 s, about 12.1 minutes.
- Self-check: Divide by 8 to turn megabits into megabytes before anything else.

**C3 (stretch).** Record a high-contrast scene on your standard and your flattest profile. Show the histograms and one graded frame. Write two sentences on what you gained and lost.
- Answer key: The flat clip shows detail the standard clip clips. Both histograms are shown. The note mentions extra grading work and possible banding if 8-bit.
- Self-check: Does the flat clip's histogram stay off the right edge where the standard one touches it?

### Common mistakes

- Trusting the built-in mic → record sound separately and sync.
- Shooting log without a plan to grade → use a standard profile or grade it properly.
- Assuming lenses frame the same on every body → check the crop factor.

### Review

R1. At 25 fps, what shutter speed gives a 180° angle? Answer: 1/50 s.
R2. How many stops are there between f/2 and f/5.6? Answer: Three: f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6.

## Module 3: Lenses and the frame: focal length, depth of field and composition

Prerequisites: Modules 1–2. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
The lens sets the angle of view, the depth of field and the sense of space. Use the numbers: angle of view from focal length and sensor width, depth of field from the formula, and aspect ratio from the finished frame.

### Tutorial

1. Work out the angle of view for each lens you own.
2. Estimate depth of field with the formula before you choose an aperture.
3. Choose an aspect ratio and shot size on purpose.

Worked example: At 3 m, a 50 mm lens at f/2 has about 43 cm of depth, doubling at each stop. Cropping 1920 × 1080 to 2.39:1 leaves 803 pixels of height.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Find the horizontal angle of view of a 35 mm lens on a full-frame sensor 36 mm wide. Is it wider or narrower than a 50 mm?
- Answer key: 2 × arctan(18 ÷ 35) ≈ 54.4°. It is wider than the 50 mm (39.6°).
- Self-check: Compute arctan of 18 ÷ f in degrees and double it.

**C2 (core).** A 35 mm lens focused at 2 m, c = 0.03 mm. Find the depth of field at f/2 and f/5.6 with the formula. Then say how the answer changes if you use a 70 mm lens at the same distance.
- Answer key: DOF = 2 × 2000² × N × 0.03 ÷ 35². At f/2 that is 480,000 ÷ 1225 ≈ 392 mm. At f/5.6 it is about 1097 mm. A 70 mm lens at the same distance gives one quarter as much: about 98 mm at f/2.
- Self-check: Does halving the focal length multiply the depth by four? Compute both.

**C3 (stretch).** Film one 20-second dialogue at two aspect ratios and three shot sizes. Write which choices helped the story and which cost you something.
- Answer key: Six frames or clips, a note on one framing that worked and one that did not, and a clear reason for each.
- Self-check: Show a frame to someone and ask what they think the scene is about.

### Common mistakes

- Using a wider aperture only because it looks cinematic → decide the depth the story needs.
- Changing lens when you should move the camera → distance changes perspective.
- Forgetting the final crop → frame for the ratio you will deliver.

### Review

R1. What does a 180° shutter at 24 fps give as a shutter speed? Answer: 1/48 s.
R2. A camera has a crop factor of 1.6. What is the full-frame equivalent of a 31 mm lens? Answer: About 50 mm.
R3. Why shoot flat or log if you will not grade it? Answer: There is no reason; the flat image needs grading.

## Module 4: Light: colour temperature, three-point lighting and metering

Prerequisites: Modules 1–3. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Lighting is colour, direction, quality and level. Know the kelvin values of common sources, build a three-point setup, shape the quality of light and use light levels to meet the exposure you need.

### Tutorial

1. Identify the colour temperature of each source and set white balance.
2. Build key, fill and backlight and control the ratio in stops.
3. Check the exposure with a meter, a grey card and the camera's aids.

Worked example: A lamp at 2 m reads 160 fc; at 4 m it reads 40 fc (two stops less). At EI 500, 24 fps, 180° shutter, f/4 needs 40 fc.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Give the colour temperature, from the table in the lesson, for: a candle, a studio lamp, midday daylight and an overcast sky. Which is the warmest and which is the bluest?
- Answer key: Candle about 1,850 K; studio lamp 3,200 K; midday daylight 5,500–6,000 K; overcast 6,500 K. Candle is the warmest and overcast the bluest.
- Self-check: Check each value against the lesson's table.

**C2 (core).** A key lamp at 2 m gives 160 fc. You want the fill at no more than half the key, and 40 fc exactly on the face from the same type of lamp. How far from the face must the fill lamp be? What is the key-to-fill difference in stops?
- Answer key: Fill at 4 m gives 40 fc. The key-to-fill difference is log2(160 ÷ 40) = 2 stops.
- Self-check: Inverse-square: one quarter of the light needs twice the distance.

**C3 (stretch).** Light the same face three ways: window only, window plus a white card fill, and a lamp plus fill plus a backlight. Photograph all three. Write what the fill changed and what the backlight changed.
- Answer key: Three frames with notes: the fill lifted the shadow side; the backlight rimmed the hair or shoulders and separated the subject from the background.
- Self-check: Compare the shadow side brightness in each frame using the histogram.

### Common mistakes

- Setting white balance to auto and letting it drift → set it by hand and note it.
- Placing the key on the camera axis → move it off axis for shape.
- Reading a reflected meter off a white or black surface without correcting → use a grey card or an incident reading.

### Review

R1. At 30 fps, what shutter speed gives 180°? Answer: 1/60 s.
R2. Doubling the f-number does what to depth of field? Answer: It doubles it.
R3. A camera has a crop factor of 1.6. What full-frame focal length is a 50 mm lens like? Answer: 80 mm.

## Module 5: Camera movement, continuity and coverage

Prerequisites: Modules 1–4. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Move the camera for a reason, keep to the 180-degree rule unless you break it on purpose, and plan coverage and a schedule so the day delivers what the edit needs.

### Tutorial

1. Choose a move and a support for it.
2. Draw the line of action and place the cameras.
3. Write a shot list, time it and count the shooting ratio.

Worked example: An 80 cm slider move in 8 s is 10 cm/s, or 192 frames at 24 fps. Six setups of 36 minutes take 3 h 36 min and give a 9 to 1 shooting ratio for a 4 minute scene.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** A is frame left of B in shot 1 and the camera is on the north side. You move the camera to the south side. Where is A in frame? What is the movement rule for a car leaving frame right?
- Answer key: A would now be frame right of B, because the characters swap sides when the line is crossed. The car should enter from the left in the next shot.
- Self-check: Draw the line and both cameras.

**C2 (core).** A slider is 80 cm long. You want a push in that takes 10 seconds at 24 fps. What is the speed in cm/s? How many frames? How far does the camera move per frame?
- Answer key: 80 ÷ 10 = 8 cm/s. 10 × 24 = 240 frames. 80 ÷ 240 ≈ 0.33 cm per frame.
- Self-check: Check that frames = seconds × frame rate.

**C3 (stretch).** Shoot a 30-second two-person dialogue in five setups. Write the shot list, draw the line of action, edit the scene and report your shooting ratio.
- Answer key: A shot list of five setups, a plan view with the line, a short edit with A and B consistently placed, and a ratio such as 12 minutes shot for 0.5 minute used = 24:1.
- Self-check: Check, frame by frame, that A looks the same way in every shot.

### Common mistakes

- Moving the camera 'because it looks good' → write the reason first.
- Placing the second camera across the line → draw the line first.
- Forgetting inserts and cutaways → add them to the shot list.

### Review

R1. At 60 fps with a 180° shutter, how many stops of light do you lose compared with 24 fps? Answer: About 1.3 stops.
R2. Which has more depth of field for the same field of view: 35 mm or 16 mm? Answer: 16 mm.
R3. What colour temperature is daylight film balanced for? Answer: 5500 K.

## Module 6: Analog film: gauges, stocks, metering and workflow

Prerequisites: Modules 1–5. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Film is a chemical capture medium with its own gauges, stocks and costs. Know the run time of a roll, pick a stock and filter for the light, test your chain with a bracket, and compare film honestly with digital before you commit.

### Tutorial

1. Choose a gauge and work out the run time per roll.
2. Match stock and filter to the light and compute the exposure index.
3. Test with a grey-card bracket, then plan the roll and the processing route.

Worked example: A Super 8 cartridge at 18 fps is 200 s. 500T in daylight with an 85 filter is EI 320, about two-thirds of a stop slower than 500.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** How long does a 50 ft Super 8 cartridge (3600 frames) run at 18 fps and at 24 fps? How long does 1,000 ft of 35 mm run at 24 fps?
- Answer key: 3600 ÷ 18 = 200 s (3 min 20 s). 3600 ÷ 24 = 150 s (2 min 30 s). 1,000 ft × 16 = 16,000 frames; ÷ 24 ≈ 667 s ≈ 11.1 min.
- Self-check: Frames divided by frame rate gives seconds.

**C2 (core).** You film indoors under 3200 K lamps that read 40 fc at the face, with 500T. The camera is at 24 fps, 180°. Which aperture would you need at EI 500? Then you shoot a daylight exterior with the same stock and an 85 filter, and the Kodak EI is 320. How many stops of speed are lost, and what aperture would the same 40 fc need at EI 320?
- Answer key: At EI 500, 40 fc needs f/4. The EI drop from 500 to 320 loses about 0.64 stops. At EI 320 the same 40 fc needs about f/3.2 (a bit under two-thirds of a stop wider than f/4).
- Self-check: Use the Kodak table: each aperture step doubles the footcandles, and EI 320 needs 1.56 times the light of EI 500.

**C3 (stretch).** Plan and shoot a test roll (Super 8, 16 mm or a 35 mm stills roll if you have no movie camera): a grey-card bracket from −2 to +2 stops, then a short scene in two light conditions. Write the roll plan and, when the film returns, a note on which frames held detail and how it compares with the DSLR version.
- Answer key: A roll plan with shot lengths, a bracket chart, processed film with a note such as 'the −1 and 0 frames were best; the highlights held detail at +1', and a side-by-side with a DSLR frame of the same scene.
- Self-check: Does the note say how many stops of range held detail?

### Common mistakes

- Skipping the test roll → always bracket once.
- Using the wrong stock for the light with no filter → check balance and index first.
- Forgetting processing and scanning in the budget → price all three before you buy.

### Review

R1. At 25 fps, which shutter speed gives 180°? Answer: 1/50 s.
R2. A lamp at 2 m reads 160 fc. How much does it read at 4 m? Answer: 40 fc.
R3. What is the 180-degree rule? Answer: Keep the camera on one side of the line between two characters so their screen positions stay consistent.

## Module 7: Analog video, telecine and the hybrid finish

Prerequisites: Modules 1–6. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Analog also means tape. Understand scan lines and fields, the quality limits of VHS, Hi8 and Betacam, how film is converted to video with pulldown, and how to run a hybrid ingest, grade and delivery pipeline.

### Tutorial

1. Learn the structure of an interlaced frame.
2. Work out the frame-rate conversion for your delivery.
3. Set up ingest, backup and a common timeline.

Worked example: Four film frames become five video frames in 2:3 pulldown. A 100-minute film at 24 fps plays in 96 minutes at 25 fps. A 2K scan of a Super 8 cartridge is about 32 GB.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Write the field pattern for 2:3 pulldown for frames A B C D. How long does a 90-minute film shot at 24 fps run when shown at 25 fps?
- Answer key: AA BBB CC DDD (ten fields, five frames). 90 × 24 ÷ 25 = 86.4 minutes, i.e. 86 min 24 s.
- Self-check: Count the fields: 2+3+2+3 = 10.

**C2 (core).** VHS gives about 320 pixels across; S-VHS gives 560; HD is 1920. What share of HD width is each? A 2K 10-bit DPX scan frame (2048 × 1080 × 4 bytes) of 7200 frames (two Super 8 cartridges) is how many GB?
- Answer key: 320 ÷ 1920 = 16.7%. 560 ÷ 1920 = 29.2%. 8,847,360 B × 7200 = 63.7 GB (about 64 GB).
- Self-check: Divide by 1920 for share and multiply by frame count for storage.

**C3 (stretch).** Shoot a 20-second scene on your DSLR and one other analog or hybrid capture (film, Hi8 or VHS). Ingest both, conform the frame rate, grade to one look and write a one-page report on what matched and what could not be matched.
- Answer key: Two clips in one timeline at one frame rate, with a graded look and a report that lists at least three differences such as grain, resolution, colour and motion.
- Self-check: Do both clips play at the same speed, and can you point to one difference you could not remove?

### Common mistakes

- Ignoring frame-rate conversion → decide the delivery rate first.
- Cutting interlaced tape into a progressive project without deinterlacing → deinterlace.
- Throwing away original scans after grading → keep and back up the originals.

### Review

R1. How long does a 50 ft Super 8 cartridge run at 18 fps? Answer: 3 minutes 20 seconds.
R2. What does a fill light do, and how bright is it compared with the key? Answer: It softens shadows; usually up to half the key.
R3. What shooting ratio do you have if you shoot 30 minutes for a 5-minute scene? Answer: 6 to 1.

## Capstone: Same scene, two capture paths: a documented test shoot

Shoot one scene on a digital camera and an analog format, from one plan. Defend every choice with a stop count, a light level or a test, and report honestly on what matched and what did not.

**Deliverable:** Write the plan: frame rate, shutter angle, lenses, shot list, light, stock, roll plan.; Test the chain, rehearse on digital, then shoot film.; Finish both versions and write the comparison report..

**Rubric**

- Plan: Write the plan with five or more setups, a plan view with the line of action, the lighting plan with kelvins and footcandles, exposure maths and the roll plan with a cost estimate. Passing result: Every number is worked; the line is drawn; the roll plan fits the cartridge or magazine with spare.
- Shoot: Test the analog chain with a grey-card bracket, rehearse on digital, and shoot both versions with slates and logs. Passing result: Bracket results logged; both versions shot from the same shot list; matched shutter angle and framing.
- Finish and report: Ingest, conform, grade and cut both versions, fill the comparison table with evidence, and write the one-to-two-page report. Passing result: Two versions at one frame rate; a table with frame grabs; a decision statement; at least one honest mismatch.

**Pass condition:** All five rubric rows are met and the report ends with a decision for your next project.

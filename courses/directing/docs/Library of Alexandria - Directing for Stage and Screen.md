# Library of Alexandria: Directing for stage and screen

Level: Intermediate. Created 21/10/2026.

Learn to direct a short scene for stage and for camera: read the script, work with actors using Stanislavski's tools, stage and shoot it, cut it for rhythm, and plan the space, designers and the last weeks before opening.

## Module 1: Reading the play and finding your idea

Prerequisites: None. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
A director leads the whole production, so the first work is reading the play closely enough to cut it into scenes and French scenes, finding a short theme, naming the audience and turning the idea into a look. The same steps apply on screen, with different departments.

### Tutorial

1. Read the play twice before deciding anything and note your first pictures.
2. Cut a scene into French scenes and name each want.
3. Write the theme as a one-line motto and the audience in one sentence.
4. List setting, character, lighting and composition choices for stage and screen.

Worked example: A hospital waiting-room scene is cut into four French scenes, given the theme 'the news arrives whether we are ready or not', and designed once as a bare stage and once as low-key screen shots. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Take a scene from any play and mark every entrance and exit. Write the French scenes.
- Answer key: A list of French scenes, each starting at an entrance or exit.
- Self-check: Does every change of who is on stage start a new French scene?

**C2 (core).** Write a one-line theme and a one-sentence target audience for a play, and list one change you would make for that audience.
- Answer key: A short theme line, an audience sentence with an age window, and one concrete change.
- Self-check: Could someone else repeat the theme from memory?

**C3 (stretch).** Write the look of one scene for stage and for screen (setting, character, lighting, composition), four lines each.
- Answer key: Eight lines, each tied to the theme.
- Self-check: Does every choice have a reason that points back to the theme?

### Common mistakes

- Treating the first picture in your head as the only reading → reread and note alternatives.
- Writing a theme as a lecture → shorten it to a motto the audience can work out.
- Using stereotypes about an audience → ask the venue and the people.

## Module 2: Working with actors I: given circumstances, the magic 'if' and objectives

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Actors work from a believable world and an active want. Give the world as given circumstances, set the imagination moving with an honest 'if', divide the scene into a few units, and name an objective for each as a verb. On screen keep the objective and change the size.

### Tutorial

1. Write who, what, when and where for each character.
2. Ask one honest 'if' question per beat and ask for an action first.
3. Divide the scene into three or four units and name each objective as a verb with a partner.
4. Run the beat for a large room and for a close-up.

Worked example: The waiting-room scene is divided into four units with the verbs delay, send away, get told, and face. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Write who, what, when and where for one character, plus two 'if' questions.
- Answer key: Four answers and two 'if' questions an actor could answer with an action.
- Self-check: Could the actor answer each 'if' by doing something?

**C2 (core).** Divide a two-page scene into units. Name each objective with a verb, a partner and a physical action.
- Answer key: Three or four units, each with a verb, partner and action.
- Self-check: Is each objective aimed at another person and active?

**C3 (stretch).** Rehearse one beat twice, once for a large room and once for a close-up, and record the difference.
- Answer key: A note that names the objective as unchanged and lists what changed.
- Self-check: Did the objective stay the same in both versions?

### Common mistakes

- Asking an actor to 'feel sad' → give a circumstance and an action instead.
- Dividing a scene into too many tiny steps → keep a few large units.
- Choosing an objective that is a result (to be moving) → choose an action toward a person.

### Review

R1. What is a French scene? Answer: A section that begins and ends when a character enters or leaves.
R2. What is a theme in Garcia's account? Answer: The short, simple, strong message that the audience carries away.

## Module 3: Working with actors II: communion, the through-line and the rehearsal room

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 2. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Connect actors to each other through communion and adaptation, tie units to a super-objective and through-line, and run a rehearsal room with calm notes. On film the director carries the whole line so the actor can play the moment.

### Tutorial

1. Run a unit three times with the same objective and a new adaptation.
2. Write a super-objective, a through-line sentence and a counteraction.
3. Plan one rehearsal day with two notes in praise-correct-praise form.
4. Plan a half-day shoot in setups.

Worked example: Dee's unit 'send away' is replayed with three adaptations; her super-objective is 'to stay in charge of how her life changes'. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Run one unit three times with the same objective and a different adaptation, and note the effect.
- Answer key: Three runs and a note naming the version that changed the partner most.
- Self-check: Did the objective stay constant while the adaptation changed?

**C2 (core).** Write a super-objective, a through-line sentence and a counteraction for the main role in a scene, and check each unit.
- Answer key: Three lines and a unit check with any unit cut or reworded.
- Self-check: Does every unit serve the line?

**C3 (stretch).** Plan one rehearsal day and one half-day shoot for the same scene, with notes in praise-correct-praise form.
- Answer key: A day plan and a setup list, each with a note.
- Self-check: Does each note give one thing to try?

### Common mistakes

- Telling the actor what to feel → give an action and a partner.
- Letting a unit drift from the through-line → test it against the super-objective.
- Giving five notes at once → pick one.

### Review

R1. Name Garcia's four given-circumstance questions. Answer: Who, what, when and where.
R2. Why name an objective as a verb? Answer: A verb gives more push to action.

## Module 4: Staging the scene: focus, distance, crosses and the frame

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 3. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Garcia's stage language covers focus areas, body positions, levels, distance, crosses and composition. These tell the audience about status, relationships and secrets. On screen the same ideas become angle, shot size, depth and screen direction.

### Tutorial

1. Name the parts of the stage and the focus order for your space.
2. Stage a silent scene using levels, distance and a cross.
3. Set a stage picture with a visual apex.
4. Rewrite the blocking as three camera shots with a line.

Worked example: The waiting-room picture uses a row of chairs, a ten-foot distance and the nurse on a step as apex; the screen version uses thirds and a line between Dee and Hal. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Stage a silent scene in which a character gains or loses status using only position and posture.
- Answer key: A scene with a clear beginning, middle and end and at least one moment where posture overrides position.
- Self-check: Can a watcher say who gained or lost status without any words?

**C2 (core).** Stage a silent scene that uses levels, distance and a cross, and justify each movement.
- Answer key: A scene with a justified level, a distance change and one cross with a reason.
- Self-check: Does each movement have a stated reason?

**C3 (stretch).** Rewrite the blocking of one moment as three camera shots with a line and a note on what each replaces.
- Answer key: Three shots, a line and a stage-device note for each.
- Self-check: Do the looks match across the three shots?

### Common mistakes

- Standing actors in a line → find a triangle.
- Using the distance guide as a law → test it on a real audience.
- Crossing the line by accident → draw it first.

### Review

R1. What is a super-objective? Answer: The aim that gathers all the unit objectives of a part.
R2. Name one tool for giving a note. Answer: Praise, correct, praise.

## Module 5: Shooting and cutting the scene: shots, coverage and rhythm

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 4. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
On screen the director controls what is seen and for how long. Choose shot size, lens and movement for a reason, plan master shot and coverage with a line, and know when to break continuity. On the stage, transitions and loops do the same work.

### Tutorial

1. Give each shot a size, lens, movement and a one-sentence reason.
2. Draw the line and plan master shot and coverage.
3. Test a Kuleshov pair on three viewers.
4. Plan two stage transitions.

Worked example: The waiting room is covered in four setups: master, two overs and inserts, which is about 160 minutes of invented shooting time for a two-minute scene. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** List three shots for a moment, each with a size, lens or depth, movement and a one-sentence reason.
- Answer key: Three shots with reasons.
- Self-check: Could you cut a shot whose reason is weak?

**C2 (core).** Draw the floor plan and the line for a two-person scene and write a four-setup coverage list and a two-setup alternative.
- Answer key: A plan with the line, four setups and a cheaper two-setup version.
- Self-check: Does every angle stay on one side of the line?

**C3 (stretch).** Run the Kuleshov test with three viewers and plan two stage transitions for a short script.
- Answer key: Results from three viewers and two transition plans.
- Self-check: Did viewers read different feelings from the same face?

### Common mistakes

- Moving the camera because it looks good → ask whether it is motivated.
- Crossing the line by accident → draw it first.
- Cutting every scene in the same rhythm → vary pace to the story.

### Review

R1. Name two body positions and their strength. Answer: Full front is the strongest and full back is the weakest.
R2. What is a visual apex? Answer: The focal point of a triangle arrangement.

## Module 6: Space, audience, designers and the finished work

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 5. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
The space shapes the staging, designers need pictures and a budget, and the last weeks need a plan for tech, dress and handover. On screen the equivalents are location or soundstage and the work with the editor.

### Tutorial

1. Draw ground plans for proscenium, thrust and round.
2. Brief each designer with pictures and a budget.
3. Fill in a budget and find break-even.
4. Plan the last two weeks and a review sheet.

Worked example: A tiny invented budget totals $870, and the half-full income of $1,500 covers it. (Illustrative.)

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Draw ground plans for your scene in the three layouts and mark one sightline problem in each.
- Answer key: Three plans with problems marked.
- Self-check: Can every audience member see the main action?

**C2 (core).** Fill in Garcia's budget headings with each number labelled as quote, estimate or guess, and find break-even attendance.
- Answer key: A budget with labels and a break-even figure.
- Self-check: Does the arithmetic add up?

**C3 (stretch).** Plan the last two weeks as a calendar with run-throughs, tech, dress and opening, plus a review sheet.
- Answer key: A calendar and a one-page sheet with scores.
- Self-check: Does the plan leave time for designers?

### Common mistakes

- Choosing the layout late → fix the space before the design meetings.
- Forgetting production rights → get the quote first.
- Giving actor notes in tech → save them for the next run.

### Review

R1. What does a visual triangle give a stage picture? Answer: A focal apex.
R2. What is a French scene? Answer: A section that begins and ends whenever a character enters or leaves.

## Capstone: Direct a three-minute scene on stage and on camera, and review both

Plan, rehearse and record one three-minute scene as a stage version and a screen version, score both against a rubric and write a one-page review with two fixes.

**Deliverable:** Prepare the five planning pieces.; Rehearse, stage and shoot both versions.; Score, review and set a date for the next attempt..

**Rubric**

- Plan: Show the script analysis, objective table, super-objective, stage plan and screen plan. Passing result: All five present and dated.
- Rehearse and shoot: Show notes from rehearsal and recordings of both versions. Passing result: Notes and two recordings.
- Review: Show the rubric scores and a one-page review with two fixes and one thing to keep. Passing result: Scores and review, honest.

**Pass condition:** All six rubric rows reach 3, or one reaches 2 with a plan, and the review is written.

# Directing for stage and screen: source notes

Built 11/10/2026 for an intermediate learner, covering stage first and screen second. Prices and rules in the sources are American unless stated.

**Read in full:**
- *Directing* (Nick Garcia, LibreTexts, CC BY 4.0, 2020): all nine chapters (what is a director, preparation, breaking down the script, design meetings, budgeting, casting, lessons in staging, the rehearsal process, the actor's process), as fetched from LibreTexts.
- *Contemporary World Cinema* (eCampusOntario Pressbooks, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): the chapters on Mise-en-scène, Cinematography, Editing and Acting.
- *An Actor Prepares* (Konstantin Stanislavski, translated by Elizabeth Reynolds Hapgood, 1936): read in full from a copy of the text. The course cites the book, not the copy. The chapters used closely were Action, Units and Objectives, Emotion Memory, Communion, Adaptation, The Unbroken Line and The Super-Objective. The Imagination, Concentration, Relaxation, Faith and a Sense of Truth, Inner Motive Forces, Inner Creative State and Threshold of the Subconscious chapters were skimmed for context and are not cited.
- Britannica entries for Stanislavsky, Granville-Barker (search excerpt plus the print version), Eisenstein and Brook, fetched in full for the first, third and fourth.

**Read in part:**
- *Prefaces to Shakespeare*, volume VI (Batsford, 1974): Edward Moore's introduction and passages of the prefaces on *The Winter's Tale*, *Twelfth Night* and *A Midsummer Night's Dream* were read from the scanned text; the Macbeth preface, other prefaces and volumes I to V were not read. The scan has OCR errors.
- Britannica on Meyerhold and on biomechanics, Encyclopedia.com on Meyerhold, media-studies.com on Eisenstein's five methods, the RSC page on Brook's 1970 Dream and the Wikipedia page on *The Empty Space*: read as search excerpts, not fetched whole.

**Not read:**
- Meyerhold's own writings and Edward Braun's *Meyerhold on Theatre*. The terms otkaz, posyl and tochka come from a student study site and are marked unverified.
- Eisenstein's *Film Form*. The scan we found had no text layer. His five methods are taken from a secondary account.
- Brook's *The Empty Space* (the book). We use the Wikipedia article for the four modes and the definition of an empty space.
- Stanislavski's *Building a Character*, *Creating a Role* and *My Life in Art*. Tempo-rhythm and the late method of physical actions are not taught.
- The *Contemporary World Cinema* chapters on sound and genre; the video clips embedded in the book were not watched.
- The Harvard thesis on Granville-Barker's Savoy productions (listed as a search result only).
- UK and Irish law and guidance on performance rights, licensing, fire safety, safeguarding and performer safety.

**Source disagreements and cautions:**
- The film textbook calls Stanislavski's approach emotion-first and says actors let go of the text; *An Actor Prepares* tells the actor to attend to the given circumstances and forget feelings. The course shows them side by side and says which it follows. Britannica adds that Lee Strasberg's American Method is an adaptation and that Stanislavski kept changing his approach.
- Garcia's gender examples rely on stereotypes by his own admission, and his claim that over 70 percent of communication is non-verbal has no source. The course warns about both.
- Garcia's distance guide (feet between actors) is one teacher's rule of thumb. Distances vary by culture.
- The course's Britannica entries disagree about when biomechanics began (Hedda Gabler, 1906, or the early 1920s). The course says which it treats as which.
- Britannica criticises Eisenstein's *Strike* for imposing a symbol that falsified the scene.
- *An Actor Prepares* is a teaching narrative with an invented class and teacher, not a record of real rehearsals.

**Limits:** Garcia's budget figures, rights fees and process are for American educational and community theatre in 2020. All worked examples (the waiting-room scene, the budget, coverage times) are invented and marked illustrative. The mappings between stage and screen devices are our inference and are labelled. This is not legal or safety advice, and fights, intimate scenes and risky movement need qualified supervision.

## Module 1: The Greenhouse as a System

Prerequisites: None. Time: 3–4 h.

### Core idea
A greenhouse traps solar heat, so its climate swings faster than the outdoors, and small houses swing fastest. Site, ventilation and measured temperature set the limits for everything else in the cycle, so measure before you manage.

### Tutorial

1. Orientation: put the long side facing south where possible. Sunlight is the most critical site factor.
2. Ventilation: natural (ridge vents with side inlets) or mechanical (exhaust fan with motorised inlet louvers). Natural ventilation performs poorly on hot days when outside wind is below about 200 ft/min.
3. Measure at plant height: keep sensors out of direct sun and away from vents and heaters. Log the minimum, the maximum, and readings at morning, midday and evening.

Worked example: On a sunny day with vents closed, record the interior maximum, the outdoor maximum and the time of the peak. A reading that matches the outdoor forecast suggests the sensor is misplaced.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Draw a site plan showing north, the sun's path, the prevailing wind, and where the vents and inlets go.
- Answer key: Long axis oriented so the long side faces south. Ridge vents at the top, inlets low on the opposite side so air crosses the house. Prevailing wind marked.
- Self-check: Compare against the model plan. Pass if orientation, vent position, inlet position and wind direction are all present.

**C2 (core).** Run a 14-day minimum/maximum log with three time-of-day readings per day.
- Answer key: 14 rows, each with a minimum, a maximum and three timed readings.
- Self-check: Count the rows (must be 14). Every row has a minimum below its maximum and time stamps.

**C3 (stretch).** On two sunny days, take afternoon readings with vents closed, then open. Write one paragraph stating a vent trigger temperature.
- Answer key: A vented afternoon reading lower than the sealed reading, the difference recorded, and a trigger temperature justified by your numbers.
- Self-check: Rubric: names both readings, the difference, and a trigger tied to your data.

### Common mistakes

- Sensor beside a vent or in direct sun: move it to plant height and shade it.
- Using outdoor weather as the greenhouse climate: log inside the house.
- Assuming vents alone will cool the house in hot weather: plan shade or evaporative cooling (Module 5).

## Module 2: The Year Cycle: Planning and Succession

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 3–4 h.

### Core idea
A year-round greenhouse is a sequence of crops, each timed to the season's light and heat. Plan backwards from the harvest date, not forwards from the seed packet.

### Tutorial

1. Find your last spring and first autumn frost dates from your local extension office.
2. Plan backwards: transplant date = harvest target minus days to maturity; sow date = transplant date minus weeks in the tray. Weeks in tray (UAF): broccoli 4–6, cabbage 4–6, peppers 6–7, tomatoes 7–9, cucumbers 3.
3. Divide the year into four blocks: winter (low light, heating needed), spring (rapid growth, ventilation rising), summer (cooling and shade) and autumn (sow for winter).

Worked example: Tomatoes, transplant date 15 May, 8 weeks in the tray. Sow date = 15 May minus 56 days = 20 March. Illustrative: the transplant date is set to the last frost date.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Peppers are transplanted on 1 June. Calculate the sow-date range using 6–7 weeks in the tray.
- Answer key: 13 April to 20 April (1 June minus 42 days = 20 April; minus 49 days = 13 April).
- Self-check: Compare your range with the key. Pass if both ends match.

**C2 (core).** Build a 12-month calendar with at least three crops in each seasonal block, showing sow, transplant and harvest dates.
- Answer key: 12 monthly rows, at least 12 crops in total, every crop showing three dates.
- Self-check: Rubric: every crop has three consistent dates (sow before transplant before harvest) and each season has a crop.

**C3 (stretch).** Plan three sowings of one fast crop, 21 days apart, for a continuous harvest. State each sow date and harvest window.
- Answer key: Three sow dates exactly 21 days apart, with harvest windows that do not overlap.
- Self-check: Check the gaps between sow dates (21 days each) and that the harvest windows do not overlap.

### Common mistakes

- Counting from today instead of from the frost date: recompute from the frost dates.
- Sowing everything at once: stagger sowings so the harvest is continuous.
- Forgetting autumn sowings for winter greens: add an autumn block to the calendar.

### Review

R1. Why does a small greenhouse swing in temperature faster than a large one? Answer: Its air volume is small, so opening a door changes the temperature quickly.
R2. Which way should the long side of the greenhouse face? Answer: South.

## Module 3: Seed Starting and Transplanting

Prerequisites: Modules 1–2. Time: 3–4 h.

### Core idea
Germination needs warmth, moisture and sometimes light. Seedlings need light close to the source and must move on when their first true leaves appear.

### Tutorial

1. Tray and mix: break up clumps before filling. Sow small seeds in rows 1–2 inches apart and 1/8–1/4 inch deep, at about twice the seed diameter.
2. Warmth and moisture: most seeds germinate best at 65–75°F. Cover with a humidity dome, but never in direct sun, which overheats the tray.
3. Light and transplanting: after emergence remove the cover and set lights close to the seedlings, raising them as the plants grow. Transplant from plug cells at the first true leaves.

Worked example: Two standard trays (10.5 × 21 in) under one 4 ft fixture with two tubes. Each tray holds about 12–18 large transplants or 50–120 small ones, so expect 24–36 large or 100–240 small per fixture.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Sow one tray of lettuce. Record row spacing and sowing depth.
- Answer key: Rows 1–2 inches apart. Lettuce seed left uncovered or lightly covered, since it is light-sensitive.
- Self-check: Compare your tray with the key or a photo of a model tray. Pass if spacing and depth match.

**C2 (core).** Sow 20 seeds of one crop at 65–75°F. Record the day each seedling emerges and the total emerged.
- Answer key: A table of 20 seeds with emergence dates and a percentage germinated.
- Self-check: The log has a total and a percentage, and emergence days fall within the packet range or any gap is explained.

**C3 (stretch).** Run two identical trays, one on a heat mat and one without. Explain the difference in emergence time using the temperature readings.
- Answer key: The heated tray emerges earlier, with a written explanation tied to your recorded temperatures.
- Self-check: Rubric: both trays logged, temperatures recorded, explanation references the data.

### Common mistakes

- Burying small seeds: sow shallow and cover lightly or not at all.
- Lights held too high: seedlings go leggy, so lower the lights as they grow.
- Top layer drying out: bottom-water or mist the surface with a fine spray.
- Transplanting too late: move seedlings at the first true leaves.
- Covered trays left in sun: keep them in shade or indoors.

### Review

R1. Where should the thermometer sit, and why not beside a vent? Answer: At plant height, away from vents, so the reading reflects the plants' air.
R2. Using the 8-week tomato rule, what is the sow date for a 1 June transplant? Answer: 6 April.

## Module 4: Growing Media, Feeding, and Water Quality

Prerequisites: Modules 1–3. Time: 3–4 h.

### Core idea
Plants take up nutrients only when the pH and the dissolved-salt level in the medium allow it. Both can be measured, so they can be tracked and corrected before the crop shows a problem.

### Tutorial

1. Test your water first: have irrigation water tested by a lab. High alkalinity can raise the medium pH over time.
2. Target pH: most greenhouse crops have readily available nutrients at pH 5.4–6.2. Each species has its own optimal range, so look it up for each crop.
3. EC measures the dissolved salts in the medium: how much fertiliser is available, or salt build-up. Take five to ten samples per crop type and use the average.
4. Pick one method and stick to it: PourThru, saturated media extract, or the 1:2 dilution test. Calibrate the meter with standards.

Worked example: Three samples of pH 5.1, 5.3 and 5.2 average 5.2, below the 5.4 lower edge of the readily-available band. Check the fertiliser's acidity and the water's alkalinity, then retest. Do not change the programme on one reading.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Calibrate your pH/EC meter against its standards and log the readings.
- Answer key: Each reading matches the labelled standard within the meter's stated tolerance.
- Self-check: Compare each reading with its standard value. Pass if each is within tolerance.

**C2 (core).** Sample five pots per crop with one method, weekly for four weeks. Log pH and EC and compute averages.
- Answer key: A log with 20 readings per crop, weekly averages and a trend note.
- Self-check: Count the readings (20). Each week's average is within the species range or has a logged action.

**C3 (stretch).** Your pH rises from 5.8 to 6.4 over three weeks. Write what you would test and change, and in what order.
- Answer key: Test water alkalinity first, then review fertiliser acidity and the irrigation schedule, and retest after one change at a time.
- Self-check: Rubric: names water alkalinity, one change at a time, and a retest.

### Common mistakes

- Sticking a sensor into the medium repeatedly: use an extraction method. A direct reading is only consistent if moisture is controlled and tested on the same day each week.
- A single sample: use five to ten and average them.
- Testing dry pots: irrigate as usual first, because dry pots read high in EC.
- An uncalibrated meter: calibrate before each session.

### Review

R1. Name the two ways to calculate a sow date. Answer: Back-calculate from the transplant date using weeks in the tray, or count back from the target harvest using days to maturity.
R2. What temperature range suits most germinating seeds? Answer: 65–75°F.

## Module 5: Climate Control Through the Seasons

Prerequisites: Modules 1–4. Time: 3–4 h.

### Core idea
Temperature is set by solar gain, ventilation, heating and cooling. Which lever matters changes with the season: heating in winter, ventilation in spring, cooling and shade in summer.

### Tutorial

1. Ventilation targets: in summer aim for 1 to 1.5 full air-volume exchanges per minute, more for small houses. In winter, 20–30% of one exchange per minute is enough to bring in cool air without chilling the plants.
2. Circulation fans: small fans moving about a quarter of the house volume per minute stop warm air pooling at the top. Run them continuously in winter and switch them off in summer.
3. Cooling options: pad-and-fan cooling commonly shows a 7–10°F gradient from inlet to outlet. Fog cools more evenly, at about one nozzle per 50–100 ft² of growing area.
4. Shade: shade cloth, roll-up screens or shade paint control light intensity in summer.

Worked example: A 1,000 ft³ house in summer needs 1,000–1,500 ft³/min of exhaust capacity (1–1.5 exchanges per minute). In winter, 20–30% of that is 200–300 ft³/min.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Calculate the summer and winter ventilation targets for a 2,000 ft³ greenhouse.
- Answer key: Summer 2,000–3,000 ft³/min. Winter 400–600 ft³/min.
- Self-check: Compare with the key. Pass if both ranges match.

**C2 (core).** Set a vent trigger and a fan trigger, then log one week of readings to test them.
- Answer key: A log showing each trigger's activation times and the temperature band held.
- Self-check: Count the activations and confirm the logged maximum stayed below your set point except for logged exceptions.

**C3 (stretch).** Choose a cooling plan for a hot summer day from natural ventilation, shade, pad-and-fan and fog. Justify it with airflow and gradient figures.
- Answer key: A reasoned choice. A strong answer notes natural ventilation performs poorly below about 200 ft/min outdoor wind, and pad-and-fan has a 7–10°F gradient.
- Self-check: Rubric: the choice is justified with at least one number from this module.

### Common mistakes

- Relying on vents alone in a hot climate: add shade or evaporative cooling. Vents cannot lower the house below outdoor temperature.
- Turning fans off in winter: air stratifies and plants at the bottom get colder.
- Ignoring low-wind days: plan extra cooling for calm, hot days.

### Review

R1. Which pH range keeps nutrients readily available? Answer: 5.4–6.2.
R2. Where should the thermometer sit? Answer: At plant height, away from vents and windows.

## Module 6: Pest and Disease Monitoring (IPM)

Prerequisites: Modules 1–5. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Prevention and early detection beat late treatment. Integrated pest management combines monitoring with several control tactics, and escalates only when monitoring shows a problem.

### Tutorial

1. The four tactic families: cultural, biological, physical or mechanical, and chemical. Decisions rest on regular monitoring; inspect weekly to catch problems early.
2. Biological control: inoculation releases a few natural enemies early to establish long-term control; inundation releases many for short-term control. It works better in long crops and steady conditions.
3. Screens and airflow: insect screens add resistance to airflow, so they need more area.
4. Sanitation: remove debris and disinfect between crops. It is the cheapest control.

Worked example: A weekly sheet records date, plant ID, and whether a pest or damage sign is present. After week 4 whitefly adults appear on one bench: check the written decision rule, record it, and act only if the rule is met.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Set up a weekly scouting route of 10 plants per crop and log the first two weeks.
- Answer key: A log with date, plant ID and pest/damage yes or no, for 10 plants per week.
- Self-check: Count entries: 10 per crop per week, for two weeks.

**C2 (core).** Identify three common greenhouse pests from your own crops using an extension identification guide, and name each damage sign.
- Answer key: Three pests, each with a sign (for example whitefly: honeydew and adults that fly up when the plant is brushed).
- Self-check: Compare against the model table. Pass with 3 of 3 correct.

**C3 (stretch).** Write a decision rule for one pest: what you monitor, the threshold, the first non-chemical action, and when to escalate.
- Answer key: A complete rule with all four parts, non-chemical action first.
- Self-check: Rubric: all four parts present and the non-chemical action comes first.

### Common mistakes

- Treating only when damage appears: scout weekly so you catch the first insects.
- Skipping sanitation between crops: clean and disinfect at each turnover.
- Expecting beneficials to work in hot, unstable conditions: release them in steady conditions and expect slower results in variable ones.

### Review

R1. What is the summer ventilation target? Answer: 1–1.5 air-volume exchanges per minute.
R2. Name the three methods for testing medium solution. Answer: PourThru, saturated media extract, and the 1:2 dilution test.

## Module 7: Harvest, Records, and the Annual Review

Prerequisites: Modules 1–6. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
What you record in each cycle decides how well the next cycle goes. The year ends with a clean-out and a review that compares plan with outcome.

### Tutorial

1. Harvest log: record date, crop, bench, count or weight, quality and notes.
2. Clean-out: between crops and at year end, remove debris and disinfect trays and benches, as in Module 6.
3. Annual review: compare planned against actual dates and yields, then pick two changes and support each with evidence from your logs.

Worked example: A row reads: sown 20 March, transplanted 15 May, first harvest 2 July, planned harvest 25 June. The crop ran about a week late, so the review checks whether temperature, feeding or timing was the cause. Illustrative numbers.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Design a harvest log template with at least eight fields.
- Answer key: Date, crop, bench, count or weight, quality, notes, and at least two more fields (for example pest present or pH/EC reading).
- Self-check: Pass if the template has at least eight fields and includes date and crop.

**C2 (core).** Log one full crop cycle from sowing to the end of harvest, with an entry for every planned event.
- Answer key: A complete row for each planned event: sowing, transplanting, feeding, pest checks, first harvest, final harvest.
- Self-check: Every planned date has an actual date beside it.

**C3 (stretch).** Write the annual review, 800–1,000 words, with planned against actual dates, two changes and evidence from your logs.
- Answer key: A review that uses your logs, where each change names the evidence that supports it.
- Self-check: Rubric: planned/actual comparison, two changes stated, each supported by a logged number.

### Common mistakes

- Recording only yield: record dates too, because timing is the main thing to learn.
- Reusing trays without cleaning: clean and disinfect each time.
- Skipping the review: you repeat the same mistakes next year.

### Review

R1. Name the four IPM tactic families. Answer: Cultural, biological, physical or mechanical, and chemical.
R2. What is the winter rule for circulation fans? Answer: Run them continuously.

## Capstone: The 12-Month Greenhouse Cycle

Run a small greenhouse for one full year, or simulate one with logs from earlier modules. Plan the year, grow it, monitor it, and review it.

**Deliverable:** Site plan and a 12-month climate log with min/max readings and vent and fan triggers.; 12-month calendar with sow, transplant and harvest dates for each crop, back-calculated from frost dates.; Seed and transplant logs, plus pH and EC logs averaged per crop with actions taken when out of range.; Weekly pest scouting log with a decision rule and recorded actions, a harvest log, and an annual review of 800–1,000 words..

**Rubric**

- Planning: Every crop has sow, transplant and harvest dates, back-calculated from frost dates. Passing result: The dates are consistent for every crop.
- Climate: Logs cover the full year, with vent and fan triggers set. Passing result: At least one trigger is justified with logged data.
- Media: pH and EC logged for each crop and averaged. Passing result: Each out-of-range reading has a recorded action.
- Pests: Weekly scouting is logged. Passing result: Every action follows a written decision rule that puts non-chemical options first.
- Review: The review compares planned against actual dates and yields. Passing result: Two changes, each backed by logged evidence.

**Pass condition:** All five rubric rows meet the passing result. This earns the Black belt.

## Module 1: Why speak: ethics, communication and nerves

Prerequisites: None. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Public speaking is audience-centred communication in which message, skill and passion all matter. It must be honest in its intent, means and ends. Nerves are common and manageable with a plan.

### Tutorial

1. Describe the purposes of speaking and the process of a speech.
2. Apply the ethics pyramid and avoid plagiarism.
3. Name your type of speaking anxiety and write a management plan with a plan B.

Worked example: A speaker who feels worst while waiting to begin uses slow breathing beforehand, a rehearsed first sentence and a plan B if a slide fails.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Write one sentence for each purpose of speaking (inform, persuade, entertain) about a topic you know.
- Answer key: Three sentences, each plainly one purpose.
- Self-check: Could a stranger tell which purpose each sentence has?

**C2 (core).** Name which of the four sources of anxiety (trait, context, audience, situation) is yours, and write a three-step plan using one technique from the lesson and the NHS breathing exercise.
- Answer key: A named source and three concrete steps, one being slow breathing.
- Self-check: Is each step something you can do on the day?

**C3 (stretch).** Check a short passage you plan to use against the plagiarism traps and write the spoken credit for each source.
- Answer key: Each borrowed idea has a named source spoken aloud.
- Self-check: Could a listener tell which ideas are yours?

### Common mistakes

- Treating nerves as proof you will fail → they are common and manageable.
- Using someone's words without crediting them aloud → say the source in the speech.

## Module 2: Listening, audience and purpose

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
A speech is built for a particular audience. Listening is a skill with stages and obstacles. Audience analysis covers who they are, what they believe and the situation. A specific purpose statement says what you want them to understand, believe or do.

### Tutorial

1. Identify your listening style and obstacles.
2. Analyse an audience on three layers.
3. Narrow a topic and write a specific purpose statement.

Worked example: A broad topic, food, narrows to food waste, then to school kitchens, then to 'persuade the PTA to start a food-waste bin'.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** List the five stages of listening and give one thing that can go wrong at each.
- Answer key: Receive, understand, remember, evaluate, respond, each with a problem.
- Self-check: Compare with the figure in Lesson 1.

**C2 (core).** Write a short audience analysis for a real group on all three layers, with at least one decision it changes.
- Answer key: Demographic, psychological and situational notes plus a change you will make.
- Self-check: Does each note change something in your speech?

**C3 (stretch).** Narrow a broad subject in four steps and write a specific purpose statement that fits five minutes.
- Answer key: A statement with audience, verb and one idea.
- Self-check: Can you cover it in five minutes?

### Common mistakes

- Choosing a topic first and the audience later → analyse the audience first.
- A purpose that is a topic ('recycling') → add audience and verb.

### Review

R1. What are the three purposes of speaking? Answer: To inform, persuade and entertain.
R2. Which source of anxiety are you? Answer: Your answer from Module 1.

## Module 3: Research and support

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 2. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Research should take no more than about one third of preparation time. Judge every source on accuracy, authority, currency and objectivity, use a mix of six kinds of support, and present each piece as setup, execution and analysis.

### Tutorial

1. Plan research time and keep a log.
2. Test sources with the four checks.
3. Choose support and present it with setup, execution and analysis.

Worked example: Two sources on a health claim are compared; the recent, expert, referenced one is used and the biased blog is flagged if mentioned.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Write a schedule for your next talk with the one-third research limit and a buffer.
- Answer key: A day-by-day plan with research at most one third.
- Self-check: Do the days add up?

**C2 (core).** Test one source on the six questions and four criteria in a table.
- Answer key: A filled table with a clear use-or-reject decision.
- Self-check: Is every cell answered with evidence, not opinion?

**C3 (stretch).** Write three evidence sentences for your talk, each with setup, execution and analysis, using three kinds of support.
- Answer key: Three sentences, each with all three steps.
- Self-check: Does each name the source and explain the point?

### Common mistakes

- Reading sources you cannot judge → check authority and date.
- Dropping a quotation without explaining it → add analysis.

### Review

R1. What is a specific purpose statement? Answer: A sentence stating what a particular audience should understand, believe or do.
R2. Name the three layers of audience analysis. Answer: Demographic, psychological and situational.

## Module 4: Structure: openings, body, endings and outlines

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 3. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
An introduction gains attention, states the purpose, builds credibility, gives reasons to listen and previews the points. The body has two or three united, separate, balanced, parallel and logical main points. Transitions keep listeners with you, and a conclusion restates, reviews and closes. An outline becomes five cards.

### Tutorial

1. Write and time a five-part introduction.
2. Chunk ideas into main points and pick a pattern.
3. Add transitions and a three-step conclusion, then make five cards.

Worked example: An eight-minute talk has an introduction of 48 to 72 seconds, three parallel main points, transitions and a challenge ending.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Write an introduction with all five parts and time it aloud.
- Answer key: Five parts present and within 10 to 15 percent of the speech.
- Self-check: Do you say the thesis in the last sentence?

**C2 (core).** Brainstorm ten points, chunk them into three main points and test them against the five tests.
- Answer key: Three main points, each passing united, separate, balanced, parallel and logical.
- Self-check: Does each point serve the specific purpose?

**C3 (stretch).** Write a full-sentence outline for a five-minute speech and compress it to five numbered cards.
- Answer key: An outline with named sources and five key-word cards.
- Self-check: Can you speak from the cards without reading?

### Common mistakes

- An introduction that takes a third of the speech → cut it to 10 to 15 percent.
- Ending with 'that's it' → restate, review and finish with a device.

### Review

R1. What is the research time limit? Answer: No more than about one third of preparation time.
R2. What are the three steps of evidence? Answer: Setup, execution and analysis.

## Module 5: Language and delivery

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 4. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Spoken language is simpler and more repetitive than writing. Aim for appropriate, vivid, inclusive and familiar words, cut powerless habits, and deliver extemporaneously with a varied voice, steady eye contact and practice with feedback.

### Tutorial

1. Rewrite a passage for the ear.
2. Remove powerless habits and define jargon.
3. Practise with feedback and a recording.

Worked example: A nervous opening with five hedges and fillers becomes two clean sentences stating what the speaker found.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Rewrite one paragraph of your writing for the ear, with a concrete image and a repeated phrase, then read both aloud.
- Answer key: Shorter sentences and at least one concrete image.
- Self-check: Does the new version sound like talk?

**C2 (core).** Record one minute of your speech, tally powerless habits, rewrite and record again.
- Answer key: A lower tally in the second recording.
- Self-check: Count hedges, fillers and disqualifiers in each.

**C3 (stretch).** Plan a one-week practice schedule for a speech, including feedback questions and a video review.
- Answer key: A seven-day plan with feedback and recording steps.
- Self-check: Does each day have a specific task?

### Common mistakes

- Using long words to sound clever → use short familiar ones.
- Practising silently in your head → practise aloud and record.

### Review

R1. How many main points for a three-to-ten-minute speech? Answer: Usually three.
R2. What is the conclusion's three-step recipe? Answer: Restate the thesis, review the points and use a concluding device.

## Module 6: Visual aids, informing and persuading

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 5. Time: 3 h.

### Core idea
Aids clarify, emphasise, help memory, add interest and support credibility, but only if readable and explained. An informative speech is accurate, clear and interesting and uses Rowan's explanations. A persuasive speech picks a claim type and a goal the audience can accept, and may follow the motivated sequence.

### Tutorial

1. Choose and design aids for your speech.
2. Plan an informative speech with the right explanation.
3. Outline a persuasive speech with a claim and a small immediate ask.

Worked example: A bus-service talk uses one labelled line graph, 12 buses down to 5, with the detail on a handout.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** For each main point, decide whether an aid would clarify, emphasise or add nothing. Keep only the first two.
- Answer key: A list with decisions and at most one aid per point.
- Self-check: Does each aid have a reason?

**C2 (core).** Write a specific purpose, three main points and a concrete example for a five-minute informative speech.
- Answer key: A purpose, points and examples, with a named explanation type.
- Self-check: Is it accurate, clear and interesting?

**C3 (stretch).** Write a persuasive claim, label its type and outline it with the motivated sequence in one sentence per step.
- Answer key: Five steps and a small, immediate ask.
- Self-check: Is the ask realistic and non-coercive?

### Common mistakes

- Slides full of sentences → one graphic and a short caption.
- A call to action that is vague or distant → ask for one small action now.

### Review

R1. What does a microphone do? Answer: It amplifies; it does not clarify.
R2. Which delivery method suits most speeches? Answer: Extemporaneous.

- Deliver and review: Show a recording, the rubric scores and a written review with two fixes. Passing result: All present and honest.

- The textbook's claim that slide design contributed to the Columbia disaster is Tufte's view as reported; it is not verified here.
- Monroe's motivated sequence is popular, but the text itself says the only experiment did not find it more persuasive.

## Module 1: Conquest and the colonial state: the Scramble, the Congo Free State, Adwa

Prerequisites: None. Time: 5 h.

### Core idea
Within about thirty years of the Berlin Conference, European states took nearly all of Africa, using treaties, force, African troops and new technologies. Africans fought, negotiated and sometimes won: Ethiopia's victory at Adwa (1896) showed conquest was not inevitable. The module trains you to treat every figure as a range with a method.

### Tutorial

1. Order the main events from the British occupation of Egypt to the Belgian annexation of Congo.
2. Name several causes of the Scramble and the weakness of any single one.
3. Treat treaties as weak evidence of consent.
4. State the Congo and Adwa figures as ranges with their methods.

Worked example: Hochschild's '10 million' for Congo is derived from population decline over 1880 to 1920 with a share attributed to rubber violence. There was no baseline census and disease also killed many. Write: many historians accept a catastrophic loss of life, with estimates in the millions, but a precise figure is not supportable.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Order: Berlin Conference; British occupation of Egypt; Adwa; Omdurman; Belgium annexes the Congo Free State.
- Answer key: British occupation of Egypt (1882), Berlin Conference (1884 to 1885), Adwa (1896), Omdurman (1898), Belgium annexes Congo (1908).
- Self-check: All five correctly ordered.

**C2 (core).** Write 130 words explaining three causes of the Scramble and one weakness in explaining it by only one cause.
- Answer key: Must include three of: economic, strategic (Egypt and Suez), prestige and domestic politics, African crises, technology; and a stated weakness, for instance that economic explanations alone cannot explain why unprofitable territories were taken.
- Self-check: Three named causes and one stated weakness.

**C3 (stretch).** Explain Adwa in 150 words under the headings military, diplomatic and Italian errors, then state one thing sources disagree on.
- Answer key: Must include modern weapons and mobilisation of a large army; French and Russian supply and the Wuchale dispute; Baratieri's poor maps, divided columns and domestic pressure to act. Disagreement: troop numbers and casualties.
- Self-check: All three headings plus one named disagreement.

### Common mistakes

- 'Africa was conquered because Africans lacked guns' → many African states had guns; conquest involved African allies, disease, finance and diplomacy.
- Treating treaties as consent.
- Quoting a Congo death toll as exact.
- Presenting Ethiopia as unique and isolated; it was an imperial power itself and expanded under Menelik.

## Module 2: Resistance under colonial rule: Maji Maji, Herero and Nama, Mau Mau

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
Resistance did not end with conquest. Maji Maji, the Herero and Nama war and genocide, and the Mau Mau uprising show different forms of resistance. Each is contested in numbers, causes and memory, and each has been reshaped by African oral history and by archives opened late.

### Tutorial

1. Narrate each case in order with causes and dates.
2. Give the range and the method behind each disputed number.
3. Explain the dispute over the 2021 Joint Declaration.
4. Name an African voice and a colonial record for each case.

Worked example: Maji Maji 75,000 to 300,000 (mostly hunger); Herero and Nama tens of thousands to about 100,000 (a destroyed share of the population); Mau Mau roughly 20,000 to 100,000 or more depending on method. In each case the range is wide because of poor baseline data and politics.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Complete a table: for each event give years, place, one cause and one disputed number.
- Answer key: Maji Maji: 1905 to 1907, German East Africa, forced cotton or taxes, 75,000 to 300,000. Herero and Nama: 1904 to 1908, German South West Africa, land loss, about 80 per cent of the Herero or about 100,000 in total. Mau Mau: 1952 to 1960, Kenya, land alienation, 20,000 to 100,000 or more.
- Self-check: Each row has a year range, a place, a cause and a disputed number.

**C2 (core).** In 140 words explain why many Ovaherero and Nama reject the 2021 Joint Declaration, and name one fact supporting the claim of genocide.
- Answer key: Must include: they were not parties to the talks; no reparations language; 1.1 billion euros described as development aid; supporting fact: Trotha's extermination order (02/10/1904), the Omaheke desert, camps with high mortality, or the 1985 UN report.
- Self-check: One reason for rejection and one fact for genocide.

**C3 (stretch).** Evaluate the claims about Mau Mau deaths (British 11,000; Anderson about 20,000 or more; Elkins up to 100,000 or more) in 200 words. Say what evidence each uses and which you would trust most and why.
- Answer key: A good answer says the British figure counts insurgents killed in action; Anderson uses court and administration records; Elkins uses oral testimony and gaps in census data, which demographers criticise. A defensible choice states that a range is honest and says what data would help (census baselines, detention rolls).
- Self-check: Three sources, one method for each, a reasoned preference.

### Common mistakes

- Calling Maji Maji only 'superstition'; it was a response to specific policies.
- Treating Herero resistance as a 'rebellion' without noting Trotha's order.
- Reporting Mau Mau as a purely Kikuyu uprising or purely a freedom struggle; it was both a national struggle and a civil conflict.
- Taking one death toll as final.

### Review

R1. What was the Congo Free State and how was it ended? Answer: Leopold II's private rule; Belgium took it over in 1908 after the Casement and Morel campaigns.
R2. Why are colonial treaties weak evidence of consent? Answer: Translation, power imbalance and misunderstanding.
R3. What happened at Adwa on 01/03/1896? Answer: Ethiopia defeated Italy.

## Module 3: Pan-Africanism, diaspora memory and historical myth: from 1900 to the OAU, and the Willie Lynch letter

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 2. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
Pan-Africanism is the idea that people of African descent share a common history and interest, and later that African states should unite. It began in the Atlantic world, was carried into the colonies by students and activists, and produced the Organisation of African Unity in 1963. The module also uses a famous fake, the Willie Lynch letter, to teach source criticism.

### Tutorial

1. Trace Pan-Africanism from 1900 to the OAU.
2. Explain the turning point of Manchester 1945.
3. Compare the Casablanca and Monrovia groups.
4. Apply four authenticity tests to the Willie Lynch letter and say what real evidence shows about slaveholder tactics.

Worked example: The OAU Charter's non-interference clause responds to the Congo crisis and to fear of border change. A rule drafted to protect new states from outside meddling later protected abusive rulers from regional criticism. The African Union changed that principle. Separately: the Willie Lynch letter fails every test: no provenance, anachronistic words (program, indoctrination, self-refueling), no corroboration.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Match: Henry Sylvester Williams; Marcus Garvey; W. E. B. Du Bois; George Padmore; Leopold Senghor; Kwame Nkrumah with: 1900 conference; Black Star Line; 1919 Congress; Manchester 1945 organiser; Negritude; Africa Must Unite.
- Answer key: In order as listed.
- Self-check: 6 out of 6.

**C2 (core).** Explain in 120 words why the 1945 Manchester Congress is often called a turning point.
- Answer key: Must include at least four of: majority African delegates; future leaders attended (Nkrumah, Kenyatta); demand for independence not reform; organisers Padmore and Du Bois; links to postwar change.
- Self-check: At least four of these.

**C3 (stretch).** Compare the Casablanca and Monrovia groups on aims, members and method, then say whether the OAU of 1963 resembled one more than the other and why. 180 words.
- Answer key: Casablanca: radical, rapid political union, Nkrumah. Monrovia: cooperation among sovereign states, gradual. The OAU compromise was closer to Monrovia (non-interference, border respect, no supranational authority).
- Self-check: Names both groups and justifies the OAU's resemblance.

**C4 (source criticism).** A friend shares a post quoting the 'Willie Lynch letter' as a real 1712 speech. In 150 words explain, using the four tests, why it should not be treated as authentic, and name one real source of evidence on divide-and-rule tactics.
- Answer key: Must say it is a modern hoax; provenance: first in print about 1970, online from 1993, no eighteenth-century source; language: anachronisms such as program (1837), indoctrination (1832), self-refueling (1811) and the word lynching (not before 1768); corroboration: no historian has found it. Real evidence: Cobb's examples (mixing ethnic groups on slave ships; the 1845 tract Religious Instruction of Negroes) or the Slave Voyages database. Must not present any claim from the letter as fact.
- Self-check: Four tests named, at least two anachronisms, one real source, and a respectful tone.

### Common mistakes

- Treating Pan-Africanism as one programme; it ranged from cultural pride to continental government.
- Forgetting that Pan-Africanism began outside the continent.
- Assuming the OAU had power to enforce decisions.
- Saying the OAU 'created' the colonial borders; it accepted them.
- Citing the Willie Lynch letter as real history; it is a modern hoax. Use Cobb, the Baltimore Sun and real sources instead.

### Review

R1. Why did Adwa matter to Pan-Africanists? Answer: It showed an African victory over a European power.
R2. Name one cause of Maji Maji. Answer: Forced cotton, taxes or forced labour.
R3. Who was imprisoned after the Kapenguria trial? Answer: Kenyatta and others.
R4. What was indirect rule? Answer: Governing through local rulers, as in Lugard's Nigeria.

## Module 4: Independence movements: Ghana, Algeria, Congo, Kenya, Tanzania, Burkina Faso

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 3. Time: 7 h.

### Core idea
Between 1951 and 1990 most of Africa became independent. Paths ranged from negotiated transfers (Ghana, Tanzania) to prolonged war (Algeria) to crisis (Congo). The first generation of leaders faced new states with colonial borders, thin administrations and Cold War pressures. Their choices, and what happened to them, shaped later decades.

### Tutorial

1. Put the main independence dates in order.
2. Contrast the paths of Ghana, Algeria, Congo, Kenya, Tanzania and Burkina Faso.
3. Evaluate ujamaa with two sets of evidence and a stated criterion.
4. Explain the Congo crisis with four actors and an open question.

Worked example: Reading an independence speech: Lumumba's address of 30/06/1960 interrupted a ceremonial script, said independence was won by struggle and recalled forced labour and racial law, and so claimed decolonisation as an African achievement. Read it next to Baudouin's speech to see two histories of one event.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Put these in order: Ghana independent; Algeria independent; Congo independent; Kenya independent; Tanganyika independent.
- Answer key: Ghana (1957), Congo (30/06/1960), Tanganyika (1961), Algeria (1962), Kenya (1963).
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** Evaluate in 150 words whether ujamaa 'failed'. Give two sets of evidence.
- Answer key: A balanced answer gives failure evidence (compulsory villagisation 1973 to 1976, falls in output, economic crisis, 1986 IMF agreement) and success evidence (literacy, Swahili, national unity, no ethnic civil war), and states a criterion for failure.
- Self-check: Two sets of evidence and a stated criterion.

**C3 (stretch).** Explain the Congo crisis in 200 words with four actors (Belgium, the UN, the US and Soviet Union, Congolese leaders) and one open question.
- Answer key: Belgium's unpreparedness and support for Katanga; UN intervention (ONUC) and its limits; Cold War interest; Lumumba, Kasa-Vubu, Mobutu and Tshombe; open question: who ordered or bears responsibility for the killing, or Hammarskjold's death.
- Self-check: Four actors and one named open question.

### Common mistakes

- 'Independence was handed over peacefully'; many paths were violent.
- Treating the Cold War as the only cause of the Congo crisis.
- Writing about leaders only as heroes or villains.
- Using Algerian death-toll claims without noting the range.

### Review

R1. What was the OAU's position on borders? Answer: Respect for existing colonial borders.
R2. Which conference of 1945 pushed for independence? Answer: Manchester.
R3. What did Hola camp (1959) show? Answer: Abuse of Mau Mau detainees; it shocked British opinion.
R4. What was the effect of the Herero and Nama war on Namibia's memory? Answer: Genocide recognition and reparations disputes.
R5. Name a test for whether a document is authentic. Answer: Provenance, language and anachronism, internal logic, or corroboration.

## Module 5: Apartheid and its end

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 4. Time: 7 h.

### Core idea
Apartheid was a system of racial classification, segregation and political exclusion built by the National Party after 1948 on earlier segregation. It was resisted by mass movements, armed struggle, international pressure and internal revolt, and it ended through negotiation between 1990 and 1994. Historians argue about the weight of each cause.

### Tutorial

1. Name the main apartheid laws and separate them from events.
2. Order Sharpeville, Rivonia, Soweto, the release of Mandela and the 1994 election.
3. Treat each death toll as a record with a method.
4. Rank four causes of the end with evidence.

Worked example: '69 killed at Sharpeville.' Check who counted (police and a commission), when, and what it includes. The page we opened says later research puts it at least 91. A careful sentence reads: the official toll is 69, with many more wounded, and later research puts it at least 91.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Order: Sharpeville; Rivonia trial verdict; Soweto uprising; Mandela released; first all-race election.
- Answer key: Sharpeville (1960), Rivonia verdict (1964), Soweto (1976), release (1990), election (1994).
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** In 130 words distinguish apartheid laws from the events of resistance, with three examples of each.
- Answer key: Laws: Population Registration, Group Areas, Bantu Education, pass laws. Events: Defiance Campaign, Sharpeville, Soweto, UDF, Rivonia.
- Self-check: Three laws and three events.

**C3 (stretch).** Rank four causes of apartheid's end (internal resistance, sanctions and the economy, the Cold War's end, negotiation) in 200 words and justify the order, citing two pieces of evidence.
- Answer key: Any defensible ranking that acknowledges more than one cause, links each to evidence (mass strikes and UDF; US sanctions of 1986 and capital flight; 1989 events in Europe; CODESA) and states the uncertainty.
- Self-check: Four causes ranked, two pieces of evidence, uncertainty stated.

### Common mistakes

- Treating apartheid as only a social custom rather than a legal and political system.
- Forgetting that the TRC is not a court.
- Crediting a single person for the end.
- Using Soweto death counts without saying which.

### Review

R1. What did the OAU Liberation Committee do? Answer: Supported liberation movements.
R2. Name two causes of the Herero rising. Answer: Land loss, debt and settler violence.
R3. Who led Ghana to independence? Answer: Nkrumah.
R4. What happened to Lumumba? Answer: Removed in September 1960 and killed on 17/01/1961.
R5. What was the Cairo Resolution (1964)? Answer: A pledge to respect colonial borders.

## Module 6: States under strain: Biafra, the Derg and structural adjustment

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 5. Time: 7 h.

### Core idea
Within a decade or two of independence many African states faced coups, civil wars and economic crisis. Biafra, the Derg and structural adjustment show a secessionist war, a revolutionary military regime and an economic and political shock. Each is a field of argument about causes and responsibility.

### Tutorial

1. Narrate the Biafran War, the Derg and adjustment in order.
2. Explain why tolls are uncertain.
3. Test a causal claim about adjustment.
4. Compare mechanisms across the cases.

Worked example: 'Structural adjustment caused Africa's poverty.' Test: when did decline begin (the 1970s, before adjustment); what changed under adjustment (some macro-stability, some social cuts); what else changed (terms of trade, wars, governance). Verdict: adjustment shaped outcomes but cannot be the sole cause [CONTESTED].

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Order: January 1966 coup in Nigeria; Biafra declared; fall of Haile Selassie; Red Terror begins; EPRDF takes Addis Ababa.
- Answer key: 15/01/1966; 30/05/1967; 12/09/1974; 1977; 05/1991.
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** In 130 words explain why the death toll in Biafra is uncertain and give two reasons for the range.
- Answer key: Must include: no reliable census; civilians died of hunger in blockaded areas; wartime propaganda; difficulty separating war deaths from excess mortality.
- Self-check: Two reasons and a range.

**C3 (stretch).** Argue in 200 words whether structural adjustment was 'the' cause of the African economic crisis of the 1980s, using at least two other factors.
- Answer key: A good argument names alternative factors (oil shocks, falling commodity prices, debt, wars, policy errors before adjustment), notes mixed outcomes and avoids a single cause.
- Self-check: Two other factors and a qualified conclusion.

### Common mistakes

- Reducing the Biafran War to 'tribal hatred'.
- Presenting the 1984 to 1985 Ethiopian famine as only drought.
- Reading Live Aid as the entire story of aid.
- Treating all adjustment programmes as identical.

### Review

R1. What does the OAU principle of non-interference mean? Answer: No intervention in members' internal affairs.
R2. Who killed or ordered the killing of Lumumba? Answer: He was killed by Katangan forces with Belgian involvement; responsibility is debated.
R3. What is ujamaa? Answer: Tanzania's African socialism.
R4. When did apartheid end formally? Answer: 1994.
R5. What did Sankara say about debt? Answer: He urged African states to refuse it, in 1987.
R6. Name a cause of Maji Maji. Answer: Forced cotton.

### Resources

## Module 7: Genocide, continental institutions and recent transitions: Rwanda, the Congo wars, the African Union, the Arab Spring, Sudan and South Sudan

Prerequisites: Modules 1 to 6. Time: 8 h.

### Core idea
The 1990s began with democratic openings and ended in the Rwandan genocide and the Congo wars. Africa then built new continental institutions, faced the Arab Spring in 2011 and produced South Sudan, which fell into civil war. This module covers events to October 2026; the latest are unsettled and marked [check current].

### Tutorial

1. Order the events from the Arusha Accords to South Sudan.
2. Explain Article 4(h) against non-interference.
3. Treat Rwandan and Congolese tolls as ranges with methods.
4. Assess the Arab Spring with criteria, and mark recent events [check current].

Worked example: 'The colonial classification of Hutu and Tutsi caused the genocide.' A reasonable view: colonial rule hardened and politicised the categories (Mamdani's argument), but the genocide was a decision by 1994 leaders with immediate triggers (war, assassination, fear) and organisation. Historians weigh long-term and short-term causes differently [CONTESTED].

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Put in order: Arusha Accords; plane shot down; RPF takes Kigali; AU launched; South Sudan independence.
- Answer key: Arusha (04/08/1993), plane (06/04/1994), Kigali (04/07/1994), AU (09/07/2002), South Sudan (09/07/2011).
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** Explain in 150 words how the AU's Article 4(h) differs from the OAU's non-interference principle, and give one limit in practice.
- Answer key: 4(h) allows intervention in grave circumstances; OAU barred interference; limits: money, member-state reluctance, selective use, or suspension of states as the usual tool.
- Self-check: Difference and limit.

**C3 (stretch).** Assess the statement 'the Arab Spring failed in Africa'. Use Tunisia, Egypt and Libya and give a verdict with criteria. 200 words.
- Answer key: A good answer sets criteria (regime change, democratic outcomes, stability, rights) and finds mixed results: Tunisia, a democratic opening then rollback; Egypt, new authoritarianism; Libya, conflict and fragmentation. It acknowledges later movements (Sudan, Algeria 2019) and the uncertainty.
- Self-check: Criteria, three cases, a qualified verdict.

### Common mistakes

- Treating Hutu and Tutsi as primordial 'tribes' or as colonial inventions only; both are oversimplifications.
- Quoting a single Rwandan or Congolese death toll as exact.
- Treating the AU as a government.
- Treating the Arab Spring as one event with one outcome.
- Stating the current situation in Sudan or South Sudan as settled.

### Review

R1. What was the Berg Report and one criticism of it? Answer: A World Bank report of 1981 that blamed domestic policy; criticised for undervaluing external shocks.
R2. Which conflict is called the first genocide of the 20th century in German and Namibian statements? Answer: The Herero and Nama war, 1904 to 1908.
R3. Name two reasons for the OAU to AU change. Answer: The end of the Cold War, the experience of 1990s conflicts and the wish for deeper integration.
R4. Who was the first prime minister of independent Congo? Answer: Lumumba.
R5. What did the Cairo Resolution do? Answer: Accepted colonial borders.
R6. What is the TRC's relation to the courts? Answer: It offered amnesty for truth and was not a court.
R7. Which four tests would you use on a famous document that has no known source? Answer: Provenance, language and anachronism, internal logic, corroboration.

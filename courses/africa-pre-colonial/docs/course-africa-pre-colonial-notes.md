# Pre-Colonial Africa: how far the sources were read

Read this before relying on any lesson. The course is written from a finished working library (Library of Alexandria: Pre-Colonial Africa, 30/10/2026), which was itself built from public sources and the author's knowledge of the scholarship, not from books read in full. This build added a small number of web pages, listed below, all opened on 30/10/2026. Pages were fetched as excerpts; none of them is a full reading of a book.

## Sources
- part | Working library: Pre-Colonial Africa (to c. 1880) | all | The structure, claims, tags and challenges of every module come from it. It is a pointer to the literature, not a primary source. Its link check (30/10/2026) was by automated request; two museum sites could not be opened.
- full | Slave Voyages: Estimates, Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade | 8 | The whole table of embarked people by 50-year period and region was read. ## Lecturers
- Dossiers for Jan Vansina, Ibn Khaldun, Ibn Battuta, Samuel Johnson and Olaudah Equiano are built from the pages above, with confidence tag partial. None of their own works was read. Voiced lines are simulated, written for teaching and not quotations.

## Not read, or not verified
- Whether the Rhodes-era, Dahomey and Kongo details in the working library match the specialist books. The Zulu, Dahomey and Kongo details were partly checked against Reader and Rodney (31/10/2026 to 01/11/2026); the rest were taken as written.
- Ibn Battuta's visit to Kilwa: Wikipedia says 1330 and the working library says 1331. The lessons give about 1330 to 1331 and note that his chronology is uncertain.
- The Berlin Conference attendance: the working library says twelve European states plus the Ottoman Empire and the United States; the Wikipedia excerpt says thirteen European nations and the United States. Both total fourteen, and the lessons say fourteen states.
- Samuel Johnson's stated reasons for writing are from a biography and the preface excerpt, not from the whole book.
- Equiano's birth year (c. 1745 versus about 1747 from the baptism record) is approximate.
- Restitution of the Benin objects: marked check current in the lessons.
- Every link marked unverified in the working library (Smithsonian National Museum of African Art; the British Museum collection) is left out of the resources.
- The 10.7 million disembarked figure and the one-in-seven middle passage ratio rest on the working library.
- The lecturers' definitions and the barrier-aid answers (shared/barriers) were written from the lessons and not checked against further sources.
- The course has no per-lesson lecturer notes (shared/course-lecturer-notes.js).
- The Post-Colonial Africa course is separate and was not touched.
- Six books supplied by the user were read for the 31/10/2026 to 01/11/2026 review: Reader (37 of 63 chapters read, 26 skipped on purpose: human evolution and Palaeolithic chapters, post-1885 colonial and independence chapters, notes, bibliography and index), Rodney (8 of 14 read; the colonial-period chapters 5 and 6 and the front matter and index skipped on purpose), Oliver (all 23), Diop African Origin (21 of 22), Diop Precolonial Black Africa (all 14, read from an OCR of a scanned PDF) and Woodson (all 41). The Diop African Origin text is a defective edition with missing and repeated passages.
- Facts stated in findings but taken from outside these books (Vai and Bamum script dates, the date of Isandhlwana, the Egyptian unification date, the ancient-DNA remark, the Columbian exchange) are labelled as not verified here.
- The Reader chapters on human evolution and the Palaeolithic were not read, and Reader is a popular survey from the 1990s; later excavation reports on iron-working and Great Zimbabwe may differ.
- Over 700 works for Ahmad Baba, Diop's Meroe and Moroccan-invasion dates, and Reader's 600,000-a-year estimate of slaves held inside Africa were not checked against a second source.

- Source Discovery ingest (02/11/2026): Grollemund (PNAS 2015) and Chirikure (PLOS ONE 2014) were read in full; the Grollemund reference-list chunk was served without a line-by-line reading. Johnson, Kebra Nagast, Houdas (French), Ibn Battuta (Gibb), Leo Africanus (Pory, vol. III), Equiano (1789 Gutenberg text) and the Breunig front chapter are ingested but NOT yet read, so nothing in the lessons rests on them. The UNESCO General History of Africa volumes could not be downloaded by script. Huffman, and the al-Bakri, al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun extracts, were skipped for copyright reasons.

- Update 02/11/2026: Ibn Battuta (Gibb 1929) was read for the East African coast, the Sahara and Mali chapter and the editor's notes only (the other stages skipped as out of scope; one half-chapter on Oman was cut short on screen), and Leo Africanus (Pory, vol. III) for Book VII in full with its notes and the first chunk of Book VI. Both scans have poor OCR, so nothing is quoted from them. Johnson, Kebra Nagast, Houdas, Equiano and the Breunig front chapter remain ingested and unread.
- Update 02/11/2026 (later): the Kebra Nagast (Budge 1932) was read for the Queen of Sheba, Menyelek, Zion's arrival, the Kaleb and Justin passage and the colophon only. Budge's long introduction was not read, so the course takes no compilation date from it. The chunk holding Budge's Bible-verse index was served without being read.
- Update 03/11/2026: Johnson's History of the Yorubas (OCR of the 1921 edition) was read for Part I chapters on origins, tribes and government and the first chapter of Part II (the founders); the rest was skipped as out of scope, so the course says nothing about Johnson's accounts of the 19th-century wars. Johnson's origin theory (Egypt, Nimrod, Phoenicia) and his count of 1,060 vassal kings are not taught as fact.
- Update 03/11/2026 (later): Houdas's French translation of the Tarikh es-Soudan was read for the Sonni origins, Kankan-Mousa, Mali, Timbuktu's founding, Sonni Ali and Askia Muhammad (about 17,000 words). The reading is ours, not a published translation; nothing is quoted. The OCR is poor and Houdas's footnotes were read only where they sat in those chapters. The scholars' biographies, the later askias and the Moroccan conquest in the book are not read, so the course takes the invasion from other sources.

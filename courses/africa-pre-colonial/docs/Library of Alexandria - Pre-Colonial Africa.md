## Module 1: Reading the African past: evidence, Nok, the Bantu expansion, Jenne-jeno

Prerequisites: None. Time: 4 h.

### Core idea
African history before 1880 is reconstructed from five kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and blind spots: archaeology, historical linguistics, oral tradition, written sources and material heritage. Strong claims need more than one kind. The belief that Africa had no history before Europeans is a claim about colonial-era sources, not about Africa.

### Tutorial

1. Name the five kinds of evidence and one blind spot of each.
2. Read oral tradition as a structured source with known biases.
3. Use the five evidence tags and rewrite uncertain claims to match.
4. Explain Nok, early iron, the Bantu expansion and Jenne-jeno with the right tags.

Worked example: Claim: the Nok were the first iron-users in sub-Saharan Africa. Archaeology shows iron-working at Nok sites but other sites have early claims, and the radiocarbon plateau blurs 800 to 400 BCE. Verdict: Nok is among the early iron-using cultures [PROBABLE]; the first cannot be supported [CONTESTED].

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Classify each statement by the main kind of evidence: (a) over 500 related languages descend from one ancestor; (b) Sunni Ali took Timbuktu in 1468, according to the Tarikh al-Sudan; (c) Jenne-jeno had a wall by the first millennium CE; (d) Sundiata defeated Sumanguru at Kirina; (e) a terracotta head was found at a tin-mining site.
- Answer key: (a) historical linguistics; (b) written source, internal and African-authored; (c) archaeology; (d) oral tradition, and c. 1235 is a [TRADITIONAL DATE]; (e) archaeology or material heritage.
- Self-check: One mark per correct match. 5/5 passes. If you scored 3 or less, reread the five kinds of evidence.

**C2 (core).** A museum label says: 'The Nok invented ironworking in Africa around 500 BCE, and the Yoruba descend directly from them.' Write three sentences that correct or qualify it, using at least two evidence tags.
- Answer key: Must include: early iron is attested at several sites and whether African smelting was independent is [CONTESTED]; dating in this window is imprecise because of the radiocarbon plateau; a direct Nok-Yoruba line is unproven because Nok language and identity are unknown [THIN EVIDENCE].
- Self-check: Your answer passes if it names the dating problem and the unproven descent.

**C3 (stretch).** Compare the older single-migration account of the Bantu expansion with the current picture, in 120 to 150 words: what each claims, what evidence supports the change, and what remains disputed.
- Answer key: A correct answer describes the old model (one wave, conquest, iron-led); describes the new model (several waves and routes, interaction with existing groups); cites linguistics plus archaeology, with genetics as support; and states that early-split versus late-split routes and the role of iron are disputed.
- Self-check: Highlight each of the four required elements in your text. All four passes.

### Common mistakes

- Treating oral tradition as either perfect truth or worthless: read it as a structured source with known biases and cross-check it.
- Saying the Bantu tribe: Bantu names a language family of hundreds of peoples.
- Reading Nok terracottas as a window into a known society: the context is largely lost, so say what you cannot know.
- Assuming Africa's early cities needed Arab or Mediterranean trade to appear: Jenne-jeno predates regular trans-Saharan trade.

## Module 2: The Nile Valley and the Horn: Kush, Aksum, Ethiopia

Prerequisites: Module 1. Time: 5 h.

### Core idea
South of Egypt and across the Red Sea from Arabia, two literate states shaped the north-east: Kush (Kerma, Napata, Meroë) and Aksum. Their successors, including Christian Nubia and Ethiopia under the Zagwe and Solomonic dynasties, show a continuous, largely independent tradition of kingship, writing and Christianity.

### Tutorial

1. Narrate Kerma, Napata, Meroë, Aksum and Ethiopia in order.
2. Explain why the Meroitic script limits what we know.
3. Read royal inscriptions and legitimising texts critically.
4. Flag at least four contested or traditional points.

Worked example: Ezana's inscriptions are royal self-presentation. In a Ge'ez text he credits God for victory over the Noba, a claim about divine favour. Use them to date a conversion, titles and a campaign; discount numbers and triumphalism.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Put these in order: Piye's conquest of Egypt; Ezana's conversion; the Battle of Wayna Daga; Meroë becomes the royal burial site; Yekuno Amlak's accession.
- Answer key: Piye (c. 744 to 714 BCE), Meroë burials (c. 300 BCE), Ezana (c. 330s to 340s CE), Yekuno Amlak (1270), Wayna Daga (1543).
- Self-check: All five in order passes.

**C2 (core).** Explain in about 100 words why the Meroitic script limits what we know about Kush, and name two outside sources historians use to compensate.
- Answer key: The script is readable but the language is not understood, so few texts translate. Compensate with Egyptian or Assyrian records and Greek or Roman authors (Herodotus, Strabo, the Periplus), plus archaeology.
- Self-check: Includes readable but not understood, and two named external sources.

**C3 (stretch).** Write 150 words comparing how a Zagwe-friendly source and the Kebra Nagast would present the 1270 change, and why historians treat the Solomonic account with caution.
- Answer key: The Solomonic text presents a restoration of a rightful line; Zagwe sources (church hagiography of Lalibela) present them as pious builders; the Kebra Nagast was written for legitimacy, so it is a political text; dates and the dynasty's start are contested.
- Self-check: Your text flags the political purpose and the uncertain dating.

### Common mistakes

- Ethiopia was never colonised, so it was isolated: Ethiopia was in contact with the Mediterranean, Arabia, India and Portugal for centuries.
- Treating the Solomon-Sheba story as a lineage: it is a foundation myth.
- Saying Kush was just a copy of Egypt: it had its own script, gods, queens and burial forms.
- Taking the Lalibela dates as precise: they are traditional.

### Review

R1. Name three kinds of evidence for early African history. Answer: Archaeology, linguistics, oral tradition, written sources, genetics.
R2. Why is Jenne-jeno important for the story of African cities? Answer: Urban growth before regular trans-Saharan trade, without an obvious king.

## Module 3: North Africa and the Islamic world: Carthage to the Almohads, trans-Saharan scholarship

Prerequisites: Modules 1–2. Time: 5 h.

### Core idea
North Africa links the continent to the Mediterranean and the Islamic world. Carthage, Roman Africa, the Arab conquests and the Berber dynasties reshaped it, and the trans-Saharan trade carried gold, salt, people, Islam and books south and north. Our main written sources are often outsider or hostile ones, so reading them critically is the skill.

### Tutorial

1. Explain the shift from Carthage to the Islamic Maghrib.
2. Mark the Almoravid-Ghana debate as contested.
3. Map the main trans-Saharan trade and its goods.
4. Read hostile or distant sources critically.

Worked example: Roman accounts of Carthage (Livy, Polybius) are the main narrative for the Punic Wars. Use them for events and dates, treat moral claims as polemic, and check against Punic inscriptions and excavation.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Match each person to a place or event: Hannibal; Augustine; Belisarius; Yusuf ibn Tashfin; Abd al-Mu'min; Ahmad Baba.
- Answer key: Hannibal, Second Punic War; Augustine, Hippo; Belisarius, Byzantine reconquest 533 to 534; Yusuf ibn Tashfin, Marrakesh and Zallaqa; Abd al-Mu'min, Almohad capture of Marrakesh 1147; Ahmad Baba, Timbuktu scholar deported to Morocco after 1591.
- Self-check: 6/6 passes; 5 acceptable.

**C2 (core).** In about 120 words, evaluate this sentence: In 1076 the Almoravids conquered and destroyed Ghana.
- Answer key: Must include: the traditional claim exists; it rests on thin and late sources; Conrad and Fisher (1982) challenged it; archaeology shows no clear destruction; tag [CONTESTED]; the sentence should be rewritten as later tradition says.
- Self-check: Includes the tag and a rewritten, cautious sentence.

**C3 (stretch).** Using Sijilmasa, Awdaghust, Ghana, Taghaza and Timbuktu, sketch a trade route on your blank map and label what travelled in each direction.
- Answer key: Gold and enslaved people moved north; salt (Taghaza), cloth, copper and horses moved south; books and Islamic scholarship moved along the same routes. The labels show at least four goods and two directions.
- Self-check: A reviewer should be able to tell which goods go which way from your labels alone.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the Sahara as a wall: it was a trade and cultural corridor.
- Salted earth: a modern myth.
- Calling everyone in the Maghrib Arabs: the Berber (Amazigh) majority shaped the dynasties.
- Treating the Almoravid-Ghana story as settled: it is disputed.

### Review

R1. What is the floating gap in oral tradition? Answer: The gap between the founding past and recent memory where details thin out.
R2. Name two ways historians decide whether Aksum destroyed Meroë. Answer: Compare Ezana's inscription with archaeology; consider trade shifts and environment.
R3. Give one reason the Kebra Nagast cannot be read as a plain genealogy. Answer: It was written to legitimise the Solomonic line.

## Module 4: The Sahel and the Sudan belt: Ghana, Mali, Songhai, the Hausa states, Sokoto

Prerequisites: Modules 1–3. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
Between the Sahara and the forest, a chain of states grew around gold, salt, cattle, grain and the Niger and Senegal rivers. Ghana, Mali and Songhai are the best known; the Hausa city-states and the Sokoto Caliphate show how Islam and politics interacted. They are documented by oral epics, Arabic travel writing and local Arabic chronicles.

### Tutorial

1. Narrate Ghana, Mali, Songhai, the Hausa states and Sokoto.
2. Rank sources by distance and purpose.
3. Tag at least five disputed or traditional points.
4. Weigh competing readings of the Sokoto jihad.

Worked example: Mansa Musa had 60,000 followers and 100 camel loads of gold: reports of reports from Cairo. Say: Arabic sources describe an extraordinary retinue and large quantities of gold, with numbers that vary.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Complete the table: state, centre, a key ruler or founder, and a key source for Ghana, Mali, Songhai.
- Answer key: Ghana, Kumbi Saleh area, al-Bakri (1068); Mali, Niani region, Sundiata or Mansa Musa, Sundiata epic or Ibn Battuta or al-Umari; Songhai, Gao, Sunni Ali or Askia Muhammad, Tarikh al-Sudan.
- Self-check: Each row has a state, a leader and a source that fits.

**C2 (core).** Rank these four sources for reliability on Mansa Musa's wealth and justify in about 100 words: (a) al-Umari, (b) Ibn Battuta, (c) the Catalan Atlas, (d) the Sundiata epic.
- Answer key: Al-Umari is the closest to eyewitness for the pilgrimage (informants in Cairo) but second-hand; Ibn Battuta visited Mali later and describes the court but not Musa's trip; the Catalan Atlas is a European picture based on rumour; the epic is about Sundiata, not Musa. Credit any ranking that cites informant distance and purpose.
- Self-check: You note informant distance and purpose for each source.

**C3 (stretch).** Write 150 words on the causes of the Sokoto jihad, presenting two interpretations and saying which evidence would help decide between them.
- Answer key: A religious-reform reading (Usman's writings) and a socio-economic or ethnic reading (taxation, Gobir's rulers, Fulani grievances); the evidence includes Usman's and Bello's own Arabic works, Hausa-state records and the pattern of who joined; flag [CONTESTED].
- Self-check: Two named readings plus a source type for each.

### Common mistakes

- Equating modern Ghana with the medieval empire.
- Reporting Mansa Musa's wealth in modern dollars.
- Calling the Sokoto jihad just Fulani conquest or just religion.
- Forgetting that Sahelian rulers were deeply involved in slavery.

### Review

R1. Why did Meroitic writing limit what historians know about Kush? Answer: Readable signs, poorly understood language.
R2. Name two trans-Saharan goods and their direction. Answer: Gold north; salt, cloth, copper south.
R3. Who was Ahmad Baba and why does he matter here? Answer: Timbuktu scholar; argued against race-based enslavement; deported after 1591.
R4. What does ajami mean? Answer: African languages written in Arabic script.

## Module 5: The Guinea coast and forest: Igbo-Ukwu, Ife, Benin, Oyo, Asante, Dahomey

Prerequisites: Modules 1–4. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
South of the savanna, forest and coastal societies built kingdoms and non-state systems around art, gold, trade and, increasingly, the Atlantic. Ife and Benin are known for sculpture, Oyo for cavalry and empire, Asante for gold and bureaucracy, Dahomey for a centralised military monarchy.

### Tutorial

1. Match each state to its signature feature.
2. Reject the Frobenius claim with evidence.
3. Compare Oyo, Asante and Dahomey in a table.
4. Treat founding myths as evidence of belief and purpose.

Worked example: The Golden Stool story: Anokye summons a stool from the sky. Historians ask what the story does: it unites separate chiefdoms under a shared sacred object and was later invoked in the war of 1900.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Match: Igbo-Ukwu; Ife; Benin; Oyo; Asante; Dahomey with one of: cavalry empire; lost-wax bronzes from c. 9th to 10th century; Golden Stool; Agojie; naturalistic heads; brass plaques.
- Answer key: Igbo-Ukwu, lost-wax bronzes; Ife, naturalistic heads; Benin, brass plaques; Oyo, cavalry empire; Asante, Golden Stool; Dahomey, Agojie.
- Self-check: 6/6 passes.

**C2 (core).** Write 130 words responding to this museum caption: The Ife heads are too sophisticated to be African; they must have been made by visitors from the Mediterranean.
- Answer key: Must include: it echoes Frobenius's 1910 claim; it rests on racial assumption, not evidence; metallurgical and stylistic study ties the heads to Yoruba traditions; Ife has a long local sequence; reject the claim.
- Self-check: Names the assumption, names Frobenius, and states the rejection.

**C3 (stretch).** Build a table comparing Oyo, Asante and Dahomey on: how rulers were checked, main sources of wealth, relation to the Atlantic trade, cause of decline. Add one [CONTESTED] point for each.
- Answer key: Oyo, Alaafin checked by Oyo Mesi and Bashorun; cavalry and tribute; supplied captives through coastal ports; decline via the Ilorin revolt and the jihad. Asante, Asantehene with a council and appointed officials; gold, kola, captives; coastal forts; defeats by Britain plus internal revolt. Dahomey, strong central control and a royal trade monopoly; Atlantic trade; French conquest in the 1890s. Contested points include Osei Tutu's date, Hangbe and the Agojie, and Agaja's aims.
- Self-check: Each cell has an entry and each state has one contested note.

### Common mistakes

- Calling Benin a tribe or confusing the kingdom with the modern Republic of Benin (which was Dahomey's territory).
- Treating European descriptions of Customs as neutral.
- Assuming a king with a palace is the only form of complex society.
- Giving restitution as settled fact when positions change.

### Review

R1. Why is Bantu not a tribe? Answer: Language family.
R2. What is a heterarchy? Answer: Power spread across groups; Jenne-jeno.
R3. Name one weakness of al-Bakri's account of Ghana. Answer: He did not visit; relied on informants.
R4. Who was Usman dan Fodio? Answer: Scholar who led the jihad creating Sokoto.
R5. Why is the Almoravid-Ghana link contested? Answer: Thin and late sources; no clear destruction layer.

## Module 6: East and Central Africa: the Swahili coast, the Great Lakes, Kongo

Prerequisites: Modules 1–5. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
The Indian Ocean linked the Swahili coast to Arabia, India and China; the Great Lakes region built centralised kingdoms inland; and Kongo, on the Atlantic side, became a Christian kingdom that wrote to Europe as an equal. Each is documented by a mix of archaeology, chronicles and surviving African letters.

### Tutorial

1. Explain the Swahili origins debate.
2. Compare Buganda and Kongo.
3. Read Afonso's letter as a source.
4. Reject the Hamitic theory.

Worked example: Afonso's 1526 letter: a king via a secretary to a king, asking for action. It proves a Central African ruler judged the trade harmful. It does not prove Kongo had no part in it.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Place these on your timeline: Portuguese reach Kongo; Kilwa sacked by Portugal; Fort Jesus completed; Mbwila; Ibn Battuta at Kilwa.
- Answer key: Ibn Battuta at Kilwa c. 1330 to 1331; Portuguese reach Kongo 1483; Kilwa sacked 1505; Fort Jesus 1593 to 96; Mbwila 1665.
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** Explain in 120 words why historians no longer accept that the Swahili towns were founded by Persians.
- Answer key: Must include: archaeology shows African, Bantu-speaking communities building the earliest towns; Swahili is a Bantu language; Shirazi stories appear in later chronicles and carried prestige; mixing with Arab and Persian traders is real but does not make the towns foreign; tag [CONTESTED] about proportion.
- Self-check: Includes archaeology, language and prestige story.

**C3 (stretch).** Compare Buganda and Kongo on: source of the king's authority, how provinces were run, relation to outsiders, and the surviving African-authored evidence. Add one source limitation for each.
- Answer key: Buganda: Kabaka with appointed chiefs, clans, canoe navy, outsiders arrive late; sources are oral tradition and 19th-century visitors, so thin before 1800. Kongo: Manikongo with provincial governors, Christian court, early Portuguese relations; sources include Afonso's letters, but missionary accounts are partial.
- Self-check: Each dimension has an entry for both kingdoms.

### Common mistakes

- Believing the Swahili coast was an Arab colony.
- Citing Speke's Hamitic theory as if it described real history.
- Forgetting that Kongo took part in the slave trade even while protesting its effects.
- Dating Lukeni lua Nimi's reign as if it were firm.

### Review

R1. What was the baqt? Answer: The long-lived treaty between Makuria and the Arab rulers of Egypt.
R2. Why is Ezana's inscription trilingual and why does that matter? Answer: Ge'ez, Sabaean, Greek; shows Aksum's diplomatic reach.
R3. Which source gives the Adal view of the 16th-century Ethiopian war? Answer: Futuh al-Habasha.
R4. What is a Sahelian chronicle and one example? Answer: Tarikh al-Sudan.
R5. What was Dahomey's relation to Oyo after c. 1730? Answer: Tributary.

## Module 7: Southern Africa: Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, the Zulu and the mfecane

Prerequisites: Modules 1–6. Time: 6 h.

### Core idea
South of the Zambezi, cattle, gold and ivory built a sequence of states from Mapungubwe to Great Zimbabwe to Mutapa, and, in the 19th century, the Zulu kingdom and the upheavals called the mfecane. This is also the story most distorted by colonial and apartheid-era myths, so source criticism is central.

### Tutorial

1. Explain the southern sequence without calling it a proven line.
2. Name the colonial myth about Great Zimbabwe and its correction.
3. Argue both sides of the mfecane debate.
4. Use African-authored and oral sources on Zulu history.

Worked example: Mapungubwe to Great Zimbabwe to Mutapa is often taught as a chain. Overlaps fit a shift of trade power, but direct descent is contested; archaeologists describe linked traditions, not a proven line of kings.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Order: Mapungubwe hill occupied; Great Zimbabwe at height; Mutapa emerges; Rozvi overthrow Torwa; Shaka takes the Zulu chiefdom.
- Answer key: Mapungubwe (c. 1220 to 1290), Great Zimbabwe (c. 12th to 15th centuries), Mutapa (15th century), Rozvi (1680s), Shaka (c. 1816).
- Self-check: All five in order.

**C2 (core).** In 130 words explain how colonial and white-minority governments misused Great Zimbabwe, and how scholars corrected the record.
- Answer key: Must include: claims of Phoenician or Sheba origin; looting by Rhodes's company; the 1905 and 1929 excavations showing African construction; later political naming (Zimbabwe 1980).
- Self-check: Names a myth, a correction and an outcome.

**C3 (stretch).** Weigh the claim Shaka caused the mfecane. Give two points for, two against, and say what evidence would shift your view.
- Answer key: For: Zulu expansion displaced neighbours (Ndwandwe, Ngwane) who in turn migrated. Against: drought and famine pre-dating Shaka, ivory and slave trade from Delagoa Bay, Cobbing's critique, exaggeration of depopulation. Evidence: dating of migrations, oral testimony, Portuguese and Cape records of raiding.
- Self-check: Two points each side and a named evidence type.

### Common mistakes

- Treating Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa as a proven dynastic line.
- Repeating Shaka stories (cruelty, tactics) from trader accounts.
- Accepting the empty land story.
- Dating Great Zimbabwe's population precisely.

## Module 8: The Atlantic world, the slave trades, and the Berlin Conference

Prerequisites: Modules 1–7. Time: 7 h.

### Core idea
From the 15th century, African states and merchants entered the Atlantic economy as sellers, buyers and rulers. The trans-Atlantic slave trade moved about 12.5 million people out of Africa. It strengthened some states and wrecked others, and ran beside the older trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades. The Berlin Conference (1884 to 85) closes the period by setting rules for European claims, but it did not itself carve up Africa.

### Tutorial

1. State the slave-trade figures with their source.
2. Explain at least two competing views of the trade's effects.
3. Correct the Berlin Conference drew the borders myth.
4. Test the label pre-colonial.

Worked example: Using the Voyages estimates page: 1,477,046 divided by 3,933,989 is about 0.375, so West Central Africa was about 38 per cent of embarkations in 1751 to 1800 (our arithmetic). The leading region varies by period.

### Challenges

**C1 (warm-up).** Using the Voyages estimates page, find the total embarked 1501 to 1866 and the share from West-Central Africa.
- Answer key: About 12.5 million (12,521,354 on the page I opened); about 5.7 million from West Central Africa, roughly 45 per cent. Database figures can be revised, so allow small differences.
- Self-check: Your numbers match the page you see within a few per cent.

**C2 (core).** Write 150 words on this statement: The Berlin Conference divided Africa among European powers. Rate it and correct it.
- Answer key: Must include: a gathering of fourteen states without African delegates; rules (free trade on the Congo and Niger, notification and effective occupation) and recognition of the Congo Free State; borders mostly set by later bilateral treaties; colonisation began earlier (Algeria 1830, Egypt 1882). Verdict: partly misleading.
- Self-check: You mention no African delegates, rules not borders, and one earlier colonial event.

**C3 (stretch).** Argue a position, in 200 words, on whether the Atlantic trade underdeveloped Africa. Use two historians, one for each side, and name one limit in the evidence.
- Answer key: States Rodney's or Manning's argument and a counter such as Thornton's; notes that effects varied by region; identifies a data problem (patchy population data, no counterfactual); avoids a one-word verdict; tags [CONTESTED].
- Self-check: Two named historians, a limit and a qualified conclusion.

### Common mistakes

- Treating the Atlantic trade as the only slave trade.
- Treating Africans as either only victims or only perpetrators.
- Quoting the 12.5 million figure without the disembarked figure.
- Saying the Berlin Conference drew the borders.

### Review

R2. What did Ahmad Baba argue about enslavement? Answer: Not on race; only non-Muslims.
R3. Name the two main Sahelian chronicles. Answer: Tarikh al-Sudan, Tarikh al-Fattash.

R6. What question does the label pre-colonial raise? Answer: It centres Europe.
